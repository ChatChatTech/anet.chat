# Model Lineage Analysis: Determination and Closeness Measurement

Chen Tang, Lan Zhang, Member, IEEE, Qi Zhao, Xirong Zhuang, Jiewei Lai, and Xiang-yang Li, Fellow, IEEE

Abstract—As model modification techniques are increasingly employed to obtain high-performing machine learning models at reduced costs, identifying lineage relationships—i.e., whether one model is derived from another—has garnered significant research interest. However, existing approaches are largely empirical, lack theoretical grounding, and are often ineffective against highimpact modifications. Furthermore, none have addressed the measurement of lineage closeness, which quantifies the degree of modification between models. In this paper, we reformulate the model lineage determination problem as a question of whether two models’ parameters reside within the same local optimum of the loss landscape. Based on this formulation, we propose a simple yet effective method for lineage determination. We further explore the impact of various modification techniques on models’ decision boundaries using visualization techniques, and observe that changes in decision boundaries serve as an accurate metric for lineage closeness. Leveraging this insight, we propose a task-agnostic and modification-type-agnostic method to measure lineage closeness by computing the mean adversarial distance from data points to decision boundaries and the matching rate of data point predictions. To reduce computational overhead, we design an efficient sampling strategy for data point selection. Extensive experiments demonstrate that our approach achieves 100% accuracy in model lineage determination and provides precise, quantitative measurements of lineage closeness across a wide range of modification scenarios.

Index Terms—model lineage determination, model lineage closeness, model copyright protection

The widespread adoption of model modification techniques raises important new questions: How can we determine whether one model is derived from another? And how can we quantify the degree of modification between two models? To address these questions, we introduce the concept of model lineage, defined as the existence of a modification relationship between models. To further characterize this relationship, we propose the notion of model lineage closeness, which quantifies the degree of modification. A higher lineage closeness indicates a smaller degree of change between models, while a lower value implies a more substantial modification.

Determining model lineage and measuring lineage closeness are essential tasks with both practical and theoretical significance. On the practical side, model lineage determination supports a range of downstream applications, most notably in intellectual property protection. By identifying whether a given model is derived from another, we can trace its origin and detect potential infringements, thereby safeguarding proprietary model development. On the theoretical side, measuring model lineage closeness provides valuable insights into the nature and extent of model modifications. This metric allows us to quantitatively assess the impact of various model modification techniques—such as fine-tuning or pruning—on model behavior and performance. As a result, it serves as a powerful tool for advancing our understanding of neural networks and their adaptability under different modification strategies.

Determining model lineage and measuring model lineage closeness are non-trivial tasks, primarily due to two key challenges. The diversity and complexity of model modification techniques make lineage determination highly challenging. Unlike traditional notions of similarity based on training data or architecture, model lineage is defined by the modification relationship between models. Accurately characterizing such relationships is difficult, especially given the wide range of possible modifications. Moreover, models trained independently for the same task can achieve similar performance, despite having no lineage relationship, leading to potential misclassification in lineage determination. Recently, several works [1]–[6] have explored this problem. A key insight from these works is that models sharing a lineage tend to exhibit more similar decision boundaries, while unrelated models display significantly different ones. Based on this observation, these methods generate data points near the decision boundaries and use model predictions on these points as indicators of lineage. If two models produce identical outputs on these boundaryadjacent points, they are considered to have a lineage relationship. However, this approach has notable limitations. Even minor modifications can lead to shifts in decision boundaries, altering model predictions and resulting in incorrect lineage determinations. In addition, transfer learning can change the model’s output dimension, rendering these methods inapplicable as they rely on consistent output dimensions across models. There is currently no effective metric for quantifying model lineage closeness. Different types of modifications alter models to varying degrees. Intuitively, a minor modification implies high lineage closeness, while a more extensive modification results in lower closeness. However, accurately capturing the degree of such modifications remains an open problem, and no general-purpose metric exists for lineage closeness.

To address the aforementioned challenges, we begin by investigating six widely-used model modification techniques, classifying them according to whether the modified model retains the original task and whether the modification is performed in-place. Our analysis reveals that models with a lineage tend to reside within the same locally optimal solution of the loss function, whereas models without such lineage typically do not. Building on this observation, we reformulate the problem of model lineage determination as a question of whether two models share the same local optimum. We approach this by interpolating between the model parameters and analyzing the resulting changes in performance. Subsequently, we visualize and analyze how different modification types affect model decision boundaries. Our findings suggest that the divergence in decision boundaries provides a fine-grained and reliable metric for assessing lineage closeness. Based on this insight, we propose a task-agnostic and modification-type-agnostic metric for measuring model closeness. Our approach integrates two key components: the mean adversarial distance, which quantifies the average perturbation required to shift data points to the decision boundary along the gradient direction, and the matching rate, defined as the proportion of data points yielding consistent outputs across models. Since data points are sampled from the training dataset rather than generated near the decision boundaries as in existing works, our method is not affected by shifts of decision boundaries and is applicable to any modification type, i.e. modification-type agnostic. To further extend lineage closeness measurement to models trained on different tasks, we introduce an indirect measurement strategy that aligns models with inconsistent output dimensionalities, i.e. task-agnostic. Finally, we develop an efficient data sampling strategy that significantly reduces computational costs while maintaining high accuracy in measuring lineage closeness.

In summary, our main contributions are three-fold: 1). This enables robust comparison across a wide spectrum of models, regardless of their structural or functional differences. 3). Comprehensive Benchmark and Empirical Validation: We construct an extensive benchmark comprising 136 models derived from 13 distinct types of model modifications. Based on this benchmark, our method achieves 100% accuracy in lineage determination and provides fine-grained, reliable measurement of model lineage closeness across arbitrary model pairs. # II. RELATED WORK

Since 2019, several methods have been proposed to determine model lineage. A predominant class of methods relies on the generation of model-specific inputs, commonly referred to as fingerprints. These include adversarial examples [1], transferable adversarial examples [3], universal adversarial perturbations [4], and noise-based inputs derived from meta-training procedures [5]. The core idea behind these approaches is to assess whether a suspect model shares lineage with the model by evaluating its predictions on the set of fingerprints—lineage is inferred if the two models produce matching labels for most of the inputs. Other methods examine discrepancies or similarities in model behavior from a performance or representational perspective. For example, Li et al. [2] propose computing the cosine similarity between decision distance vectors to identify lineage. Chen et al. [7] advocate for a comprehensive evaluation using a diverse suite of test metrics. Guan et al. [6] proposes to calculate the correlation matrix between models’ outputs and compare them with a preset threshold to determine lineage.

However, existing methods exhibit sensitivity to highimpact modifications, which can induce substantial shifts in decision boundaries. Such alterations often render the previously generated fingerprints prone to misclassification. Furthermore, many of these approaches presuppose consistency in output dimensionality across models, thereby limiting their applicability in scenarios involving tasks with heterogeneous output spaces. Moreover, they generally fail to quantify the extent of modification, thereby lacking the ability to assess lineage closeness.

To address these limitations, this paper conducts an in-depth analysis of model lineage determination and the measurement of lineage closeness, as detailed in Sec 4 and 5. This analysis motivates the development of a simple yet robust approach for lineage determination, as well as a modification-type-agnostic and task-agnostic metric for quantifying lineage closeness.

# III. PROBLEM STATEMENT AND DESIGN SPACE

Before delving into model lineage analysis, we first formalize the problem and outline the design space considered in this work.

In model lineage determination and closeness measurement, we focus on two classification models: a source model $f$ and a compared model $^ { g , }$ as well as a dataset $d = \{ x _ { i } , y _ { i } \}$ sampled from the task distribution. In our primary analysis, $d$ corresponds to the original training dataset of $f ,$ but generally, it can be any dataset aligned with the task. The objective is twofold: (1) to determine whether $g$ is derived from $f$ through some modification process $( \mathrm { i . e . }$ , lineage determination), and (2) to quantify the extent of such modifications (i.e., lineage closeness measurement) from $f$ to $g .$ Formally, the problem can be described as a two-step process:

Step 1: Lineage Determination. $\mathbf { A }$ binary decision function $D ( f , g , d )$ takes the source model $f ,$ compared model $^ { g , }$ and the training dataset d as inputs, and outputs a result $s _ { D }$ , indicating whether a lineage relationship exists between $f$ and $g .$ .

Step 2: Lineage Closeness Measurement. A scoring function $L ( f , g , d )$ takes the same inputs and outputs a continuous value $s _ { L } \in [ 0 , 1 ]$ , representing the degree of modifications from $f$ to $g .$ .

To comprehensively evaluate model lineage, we consider six commonly used model modification techniques within our design space: (1) Fine-tuning [8] alters the model’s parameters by training for a few additional epochs on a subset of the model’s parameters using the training dataset; (2) Pruning [9] reduces the size of less important parts of the model’s parameters; (3) Adversarial training [10] improves robustness against adversarial attacks by training with adversarial samples; (4) Quantization [11] compresses the model by converting its parameters to integer or lower-precision floating-point types; (5) Transfer learning [12] transfers the knowledge from the model to a different but related task by training with the dataset of the related task; (6) Knowledge distillation [13] distills knowledge from the source model to train a student model. There are two common approaches to knowledge distillation: featurebased distillation requires the student model to have the same structure as the source model in order to utilize the intermediate layers’ outputs, while outputbased distillation sacrifices some of the performance to be free from the model structure.

We divide six common modification techniques into three categories based on whether the task remains the same and whether the modification is performed inplace on the original model. An in-place modification refers to directly altering the parameters of the source model, e.g., pruning, whereas an out-place modification entails generating a new model, e.g., distillation. # IV. MODEL LINEAGE DETERMINATION

In this section, we analyze the relationship between models within the solution space of a given loss function, and reformulate the model lineage determination problem as a task of identifying whether two models locate in the same local optima. This theoretical perspective informs the design of a simple yet effective approach for model lineage determination.

# A. Exploring Model Lineage

Theoretically, in complex high-dimensional spaces, the loss landscape of a neural network often contains numerous local minima. During training, a model may converge to different local optima that yield comparable performance. However, variations in hyperparameters or architectural configurations typically drive the model toward distinct local optima [14]. Each locally optimal solution can be conceptualized as a distinct basin in the high-dimensional loss landscape. For illustrative purposes, Fig. 1 presents a schematic containing two such local optima. Here, $\Theta _ { A }$ and $\Theta _ { B }$ denote model parameter sets corresponding to two different local optima, while $\Theta _ { A } , \Theta _ { A ^ { \prime } }$ , and $\Theta _ { A ^ { \prime \prime } }$ reside within the same local basin.

Models within the same local optimum generally exhibit similar performance. In contrast, moving outside a local basin often leads to a sharp degradation in performance, typically converging to random guess accuracy, i.e., $\textstyle { \frac { 1 } { n } } .$ , where n is the number of classes. As a result, interpolating between parameters such as $\Theta _ { A }$ and $\Theta _ { A ^ { \prime } }$ yields intermediate models that still lie within the same local optimum and thus retain comparable performance. However, interpolating between $\Theta _ { A }$ and $\Theta _ { B }$ traverses across different local basins, resulting in intermediate models with significantly degraded performance.

This observation implies that as long as model performance is preserved during modification, the model remains within the same local optimum.

To empirically validate the aforementioned analysis, we adopt the interpolation methodology proposed in [14] to examine models with and without lineage. Specifically, we interpolate their parameters and evaluate the resulting models’ losses on the training dataset.

Given a source model f with parameters $\Theta _ { A }$ and a compared model g with parameters $\Theta _ { B } .$ , we generate interpolated models using the following linear combination:

$$
\Theta_ {j} = \lambda * \Theta_ {A} + (1 - \lambda) * \Theta_ {B}, \tag {1}
$$

where $\lambda ~ \in ~ [ 0 , 1 ]$ is a tunable interpolation coefficient. Varying λ yields a set of interpolated parameters $[ \Theta _ { 0 } , . . . , \Theta _ { j } , . . . ]$ , each corresponding to a distinct model. We then compute the training loss of each interpolated model on dataset d. The resulting loss curves are depicted in Fig. 2, from which we derive the following key observations:

•Observation #1-1: For the first category of modification techniques, the loss landscape of the interpolated models approximates a straight line.

We first investigate whether the first category of modification techniques displaces the model from its original local optimum. Empirical evidence suggests that this is not the case. When both models lie within the same local optimum, interpolating between their parameters—such as $\Theta _ { A }$ and $\Theta _ { A ^ { \prime } }$ in Fig. 1—produces intermediate models that also reside in the same local basin and thus exhibit similar losses. However, modification techniques like pruning, although potentially degrading accuracy, do not necessarily move the model out of the local optimum. Instead, they may shift the model away from the center of the basin, such as from $\Theta _ { A }$ to $\Theta _ { A ^ { \prime \prime } }$ in Fig. 1, leading to an interpolated loss landscape that appears linear with a slope.

Fig. 2. Loss landscape between the source model and different compared models.

Subsequently, to resolve task inconsistency between the source and the compared models in transfer learning scenarios, we apply transfer learning to the source model. We then interpolate between the transferred source model and the compared model, and compute the training losses of the interpolated models.

•Observation #1-2: For the second category of modification techniques, after task alignment, the loss landscape of the interpolated models remains approximately linear.

We observe that transfer learning does not significantly alter the model’s feature extraction capability. Once task alignment is ensured, both models remain in the same local optimum. Consequently, the interpolation between them yields a straight loss curve, indicating consistent performance across interpolated models.

•Observation #1-3: For the third category of modification techniques, the interpolated loss landscape resembles that of models without lineage and exhibits a convex shape.

In this case, the loss landscape forms a convex curve, indicative of the fact that the two models reside in different local optima—specifically, similar to the transition from $\Theta _ { A }$ to $\Theta _ { B }$ in Fig. 1. The interpolation path moves out of one local basin and into another, causing the loss to first increase and then decrease, forming a characteristic convex profile. This observation suggests that the distilled model no longer shares the same local optimum as the source model. Despite the use of feature-alignment constraints during knowledge distillation, the distilled model behaves like an independently trained model and converges to a different local minimum. As a result, it becomes indistinguishable from a model without lineage. Therefore, we exclude knowledge distillation from the set of modification techniques that can reliably infer lineage. In all subsequent experiments, distilled models are no longer considered lineage-preserving.

In conclusion, distilled models are indistinguishable from models without lineage, as both reside in local optima different from that of the source model. In contrast, models modified by other techniques remain in the same local optimum as the source model, allowing lineage to be reliably determined.

# B. Lineage Determination Approach

Building upon the above observations and analyses, we propose a method to determine model lineage by assessing whether the source model and the compared model reside in the same local optimum.

In essence, lineage can be inferred by examining the performance trend of interpolated models between the source and compared models on the training dataset. If the interpolation results exhibit a substantial drop in accuracy, the two models are considered to reside in different local optima and are thus deemed unrelated. Conversely, if accuracy remains stable across interpolations, a lineage relationship is inferred.

Concretely, given a set of interpolated parameters $[ \Theta _ { 0 } , . . . , \Theta _ { j } , . . . ]$ ] of the source model $f$ and the compared model g, we calculate the minimum accuracy of these parameters on the training dataset d as:

$$
A c c = \min _ {j} \Theta_ {j} (d). \tag {2}
$$

If this minimum accuracy falls below a certain threshold, i.e., Acc $< \alpha * \Theta _ { A } ( d )$ , we conclude that the models do not share lineage. Here, α is set at ${ \frac { 2 } { n } } ,$ based on our observation that models departing from a local optimum typically drop to near-random performance levels, around ${ \frac { 1 } { n } } .$ , where n denotes the number of classes.

This criterion provides a principled method for lineage determination, enabling lineage analysis across a variety of modification techniques.

# V. MODEL LINEAGE CLOSENESS MEASUREMENT

In this section, we explore model lineage closeness. To gain intuitive insights into lineage closeness, we first employ the decision boundary visualization technique proposed in [15]. Based on this, we then design a lineage closeness measurement method that is agnostic to both the modification type and task.

# A. Exploring Model Lineage Closeness

To understand how different modification techniques affect model lineage closeness, we leverage the decision boundary visualization method introduced in [15]. Specifically, we conduct experiments across four datasets—MNIST [16], CIFAR-10 [17], Flower102 [18], and SDog120 [19]—and four network architectures: LeNet [16], VGG16 [20], MobileNetv2 [21], and ResNet18 [22]. For each dataset-architecture pair, we construct three groups of models: (1) the source model, (2) lineage models (derived by modifying the source model), and (3) no lineage models (trained independently with different hyperparameters or architectures). We repeatedly visualize regions of their decision boundaries. An illustrative example of these visualizations is shown in Fig. 3, from which we derive several key observations:

•Observation #2-1: Models with lineage tend to exhibit strong similarities in their decision boundaries, while models without lineage show substantial divergence.

This suggests that models converging to different local optima (i.e., without lineage) often develop different decision boundaries, whereas those residing in the same local optimum share same decision boundary characteristics.

•Observation #2-2: Within the first category of modification techniques, the extent to which decision boundaries are altered varies across techniques and configurations.

For example, adversarial training causes more pronounced changes than fine-tuning. Even within the same technique, different parameter settings lead to differing effects—e.g., pruning 50% of parameters alters the decision boundary more than pruning 20%.

•Observation #2-3: In the second category of modification techniques (i.e., transfer learning), task mismatch leads to inconsistent decision space dimensions between the source and modified models.

•Observation #2-4: Some regions of the decision boundary are highly sensitive to modifications, showing large deviations even with small changes. Conversely, some parts of the decision boundaries are commonly present in the source model, lineage models, and no lineage models.

Both types of regions complicate the evaluation of decision boundary similarity: the former introduces noise due to instability, while the latter lacks discriminative power.

In summary, these findings indicate that decision boundary similarity is a meaningful metric for lineage closeness, as it reflects the degree of modification between models. However, several challenges remain: 1) How to quantitatively compare decision boundaries to assess modification extent? 2) How to address inconsistencies in decision space dimensionality caused by task shifts? 3) How to mitigate the influence of oversensitive and non-discriminative boundary regions on similarity evaluation? To address these challenges, we design a modification-type agnostic and task-agnostic model lineage closeness measurement method, which is described below.

# B. Linegae Closeness Measurement Approach

The overall workflow of our proposed method is illustrated in Fig. 4. It consists of two core components: a test set generator and a lineage closeness score calculator. The test set generator efficiently constructs an input set tailored for evaluating lineage closeness, while the score calculator takes this set as input to both the source model and the compared model to compute a numerical closeness score. Importantly, the data points used for score calculation are sampled from the training set and are intentionally kept away from the decision boundary. As a result, the measurement remains robust to shifts in decision boundaries caused by different types of modifications—making the method inherently modification-type agnostic. Furthermore, to enable taskagnostic measurement, we introduce an indirect measurement strategy: models trained on different tasks are first transferred to a common task space, after which lineage closeness is assessed under a unified task setting. We elaborate on the technical details of each component below.

1) Lineage Closeness Score Calculation: Building upon Observations #2 − 1, #2 − 2,, we quantify model lineage closeness using decision boundary similarity. Specifically, for a source model f and a compared model g, we assess how similar their decision boundaries are by measuring the distance from data points to the respective decision boundaries of the two models. We adopt the adversarial distance [23], defined as the shortest distance from a data point to the decision boundary along the gradient direction. As shown in case 1 of Fig.5, for each input x, we first calculate its gradient direction by:

$$
\boldsymbol {g r a d} = \operatorname{sign} (\nabla f (\boldsymbol {x})). \tag {3}
$$

Then, we calculate the adversarial distances from x to the decision boundaries of models f and g along grad:

$$
d _ {f} = \arg \min _ {d _ {f}} [ f (\boldsymbol {x} + d _ {f} * \boldsymbol {g r a d}) \neq f (\boldsymbol {x}) ], \tag {4}
$$

$$
d _ {g} = \arg \min _ {d _ {g}} [ g (\boldsymbol {x} + d _ {g} * \boldsymbol {g r a d}) \neq g (\boldsymbol {x}) ]. \tag {5}
$$

Beyond distance, boundary similarity also requires consistent output behavior on both sides of the boundary. That is, we require:

$$
f (\boldsymbol {x}) = g (\boldsymbol {x}), \quad f (\boldsymbol {x} + d _ {f} * \boldsymbol {g r a d}) = g (\boldsymbol {x} + d _ {g} * \boldsymbol {g r a d}). \tag {6}
$$

As shown in Case 2 of Fig. 5, when this condition is not met, the adversarial distances alone are insufficient to capture decision boundary similarity. To address this, we introduce a matching rate metric to complement the distance-based measure. Let N be the total number of data points, and let k be the number of points satisfying Eq. (6). The matching rate Mr is defined as:

$$
M r = \frac {k}{N}, M r \in [ 0, 1 ]. \tag {7}
$$

A successful match occurs when the predictions on both sides of the boundary are identical for both models. Thus, a higher Mr reflects greater consistency in model predictions near decision boundaries. To constrain the adversarial distance within a valid data domain, we cap it at 1; any distance exceeding this threshold is considered a failed match. For all successfully matched points (denoted as M), we calculate the mean absolute difference between adversarial distances of the two models:

$$
\overline {{{d}}} = \frac {1}{| M |} \sum_ {x \in M} | d _ {f} - d _ {g} |, \overline {{{d}}} \in [ 0, 1 ]. \tag {8}
$$

Finally, the model lineage closeness score s is computed by combining Mr and d as:

$$
s = (1 - \overline {{{d}}}) * M r, s \in [ 0, 1 ]. \tag {9}
$$

Unlike existing methods that are sensitive to shifts in decision boundaries caused by model modifications, our metric s is designed to be modification-type agnostic. It directly captures both the geometric and behavioral similarity of decision boundaries, making it a generalizable measure of model lineage closeness.

2) Test Set Generation: Measuring lineage closeness using the entire dataset incurs substantial computational overhead. Moreover, as discussed in Observations #2−4, two specific types of decision boundaries—those highly sensitive to model modifications and those commonly shared across models—do not contribute meaningfully to the accurate assessment of lineage closeness. To address these issues, we propose a two-step test set generation process, detailed as follows:

1. Filtering invalid samples: To mitigate the influence of sensitive decision boundaries, we first generate a set of models with high lineage closeness by modifying (e.g., fine-tuning) the source model. Each data point is then evaluated to determine whether it elicits consistent predictions from both the source model and its lineage variants. Samples that fail to produce matching predictions are discarded. Next, to exclude the impact of common decision boundaries, we generate no lineage models using alternative training configurations. Samples that produce consistent outputs across both the source model and the no-lineage models are also removed.

2. Sample discrepancy data points: After filtering out invalid samples, a large number of data points typically remain. To further reduce computational cost, we sample a representative subset of these for lineage closeness evaluation. Since the calculation involves measuring adversarial distances based on input gradients, selecting data points with greater diversity helps capture variations in gradient directions, thereby providing a more reliable characterization of decision boundary similarity. However, selecting the most mutually distinct K data points is an NP-complete problem and is intractable in polynomial time. To approximate this, we compute the cumulative difference of each data point from all others, rank the results, and select the top K most distinct samples. These form the final test set $d _ { t }$ used to evaluate model lineage closeness, where K denotes the predefined number of data points.

3) Indirect Measurement: Based on Observations #2 − 3, transfer learning leads to inconsistency in the decision space dimensions, making direct measurement of model lineage closeness infeasible for models trained on different tasks. However, Observations #1−2 indicate that, when a source model and a compared model share a lineage, they locate in a same locally optimal solution once their tasks are aligned through transfer learning. Therefore, we propose an indirect measurement, approach for assessing lineage closeness between models with different tasks. This approach extends the lineage closeness measurement process described earlier by incorporating an additional step: we perform transfer learning on the source model using the training dataset of the compared model, thereby aligning the decision space dimensions. The transfer learning process only requires appropriately set hyperparameters (e.g., number of epochs, learning rate, etc.). After the alignment, we compute the lineage closeness score between the transferred source model and the compared model. This score is then used for the lineage closeness between the original source and compared models. In this way, our method becomes task-agnostic.

The effectiveness of this method is largely attributed to the preservation of the model’s feature extraction ability. Although the dimensions of the feature representations may differ initially, utilizing the same dataset ensures that the feature representations of the models remain as similar as possible, facilitating a reliable comparison of decision boundaries.

# C. Lineage Determination Approach

We introduced a model lineage determination method in Sec 4. While theoretically reliable at the design level, this white-box approach requires access to the parameters of the compared model—an assumption that may not hold in real-world scenarios. To address this limitation, we extend the model lineage closeness method presented in this section to develop a black-box lineage determination approach.

In the black-box setting, we randomly generate a small number of both lineage and no lineage models based on the source model, compute their lineage closeness scores with respect to the source model, and use these results to establish an empirical threshold. If the lineage closeness score between the source model and the compared model exceeds this threshold, we infer a lineage relationship; otherwise, the models are considered unrelated.

The white-box method offers higher accuracy, as its determination is grounded in a theoretical framework based on parameter interpolation. In contrast, the blackbox method trades theoretical rigor for practicality: it requires only black-box access to the compared model (e.g., via an API), making it more applicable in realworld scenarios where internal model parameters are unavailable.

# VI. EXPERIMENTS

In this section, we construct a comprehensive benchmark derived from the ModelReuse benchmark [2], encompassing a wide range of common model modification techniques. Using this benchmark, we demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in both precise lineage determination and quantitative measurement of lineage closeness.

# A. Benchmark and Experiments Setup

To develop a more comprehensive benchmark for exploring model lineage closeness, we extend the existing benchmark [2] to include all major types of model modifications. In our extended benchmark, we retain the original model architectures—MobileNetV2 and ResNet18—and the datasets used in the prior work: Oxford Flowers 102, Stanford Dogs 120, and ImageNet [24]. As summarized in Table II, the benchmark now consists of 136 models, including 2 pretrained models, 108 modified models (12 transferred, 24 fine-tuned, 24 pruned, 12 adversarial trained, 24 quantized, and 12 distilled), and 26 retrained models. Each modified model is derived from one of the transferred models using a specific modification technique, as detailed in the “Configuration” column of Table II. Furthermore, there are 26 retrained models that have been trained from scratch.

For evaluation, we compare our method against several state-of-the-art baselines: CAE [3], ModelDiff [2], SAC [6], MeTaFinger [5], and DEEPJUDGE [7]. Specifically, for CAE, we generate 3 lineage models and take 2 no lineage models to produce 100 conferrable adversarial examples as fingerprints. For ModelDiff, we use 100 test inputs to compare behavior patterns across models. For SAC, 100 test inputs are used to compute correlation distances. For MeTaFinger, 100 fingerprint samples are generated via meta-training. For DEEPJUDGE, 100 PGD-based fingerprints are created to determine lineage. The lineage determination threshold is uniformly set to the lowest among all baselines, i.e., 0.5. All experiments are conducted on a Linux server equipped with a Tesla P100 GPU. The implementations are based on PyTorch 1.5 and Python 3.7.

# B. Correctness

We compare the correctness of our method with baselines using both white-box and black-box approaches. The white-box method, Ours-W, applies the lineage determination method from Sec 4, interpolating the parameters of the source and compared models, and evaluating the accuracy of the interpolated results. The black-box method, Ours-B, generates 100 samples for lineage determination, setting a threshold δ for lineage identification. After generating 4 lineage and 2 non lineage models for each source model and calculating the lineage closeness score, we set δ = 0.35 which can well distinguish generated lineage models and no lineage models. The set of models (24 models) used to determine the threshold differs from the evaluation model set (198 models).

Using the benchmark, we generate 198 model pairs—112 with lineage and 86 without—and evaluate the determination accuracy of both our method and baselines. As shown in Table III, our method achieves an impressive accuracy of 95% and 100%, outperforming CAE (84%), ModelDiff (67%), SAC (60%), MeTaFinger (85%), and DEEPJUDGE (86%). In the black-box approach, threshold selection is somewhat empirical, resulting in 9 incorrect lineage determinations for distillation models, but all other model types are correctly identified. The white-box approach performs accurately for all models. In contrast, baselines often misidentify lineage, particularly when the source model undergoes substantial modifications like pruning and adversarial training. ModelDiff and SAC perform well with lineage models but show higher incorrect determination for non lineage models.

Compared to baselines, our method can determine lineage across different tasks and measure lineage closeness between any two models. In contrast, baselines fail to distinguish models with different modifications, limiting their accuracy in both lineage determination and closeness measurement.

# C. Efficiency

We compare the time efficiency of our method with baseline approaches, considering the overhead from model generation, test set construction, and lineage determination. The results are presented in Tab.IV.

During model generation, our white-box approach takes about 1 second, as it only requires interpolating between the parameters of the source and compared models (25 times). The lineage determination phase is slightly more time-consuming, as it involves evaluating the accuracy of each interpolated model on the training dataset. Despite this, the white-box method achieves 100% accuracy with acceptable total overhead.

Our black-box approach generates models with high lineage closeness through fine-tuning and pruning, which is more efficient than the methods used in CAE and MeTaFinger, resulting in lower model generation time. Unlike the baselines, which generate 100 samples meeting predefined criteria (e.g., adversarial examples), our method selects the most representative 100 samples from the training dataset for lineage determination. This selection adds roughly 800 seconds of overhead. Additionally, the black-box approach performs fine-grained lineage analysis, requiring about 10 seconds for lineage determination. While test set generation introduces extra overhead, both the model and test set can be pregenerated and reused. As a result, the actual lineage evaluation phase takes only about 10 seconds, which is practical. Notably, our black-box method can assess lineage closeness within this timeframe, a capability not supported by baseline methods.

# E. Robustness against Adversarial Counter-Forensics

A potential concern is whether adversaries can evade detection through ”Adversarial Removal”. We argue that our method is intrinsically robust due to the geometric properties of neural networks. In the white-box setting, ”removing” lineage requires moving the model out of its original local basin. Empirical evidence suggests that traversing the high-loss barrier to a new basin incurs computational costs comparable to training from scratch, disincentivizing such attacks; In the black-box setting, our metric captures global boundary shifts. If an adversary heavily distorts the decision boundary to hide lineage, our metric will correctly report a low ”Closeness Score.” This is not a detection failure but an accurate quantification of the geometric distance resulting from the attack.

# F. Ablation Study

We conduct an ablation study to evaluate the effectiveness of our method in test set generation and lineage closeness measurement, using two datasets: Oxford Flower 102 (Flower102) and Stanford Dogs 120 (SDog120).

First, we evaluate our method’s effectiveness in filtering invalid samples. As shown in Fig.9, filtering invalid samples improves matching rates and reduces mean distances for models with lineage. In contrast, models without lineage see a decrease in matching rate and an increase in mean distance. This shows that the gap in closeness between models with and without lineage is more pronounced after invalid sample removal, highlighting our method’s precision.

Next, we examine whether sampling discrepancies affect the results. As Fig.10 shows, there are no significant differences in matching rates or mean distances before and after sampling data points, confirming that our sampling method is unbiased.

Finally, we assess the precision of our lineage closeness measurement. As seen in Fig.11, filtering invalid samples enhances the model lineage’s discriminative ability. Models with lineage show higher lineage closeness, while those without lineage show lower closeness. Additionally, lineage closeness remains stable despite sampling discrepancies, confirming the effective of our method.

# VII. CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS

In this paper, we explore the principles of model lineage determination and lineage closeness and design a model determination method and a model lineage closeness measurement method based on the local optimal solution of the loss function and the decision boundary visualization, respectively. Our comprehensive experiments show that our methods provide practical lineage determination and lineage closeness measurement solutions.

However, our framework presents certain limitations. First, extending to generative modalities (e.g., Diffusion Models and LLMs) faces challenges: the white-box ”same basin” hypothesis may be geometrically unstable or computationally prohibitive for such models, and the black-box metric lacks natural decision boundaries for generation tasks. Second, regarding data dependency, while our method primarily utilizes training data for its high-margin stability, deploying it with proxy datasets requires additional filtering of low-confidence samples to mitigate boundary noise. Future work will explore adapting these geometric principles to the continuous latent spaces of generative models and enhancing robustness with limited data access.
