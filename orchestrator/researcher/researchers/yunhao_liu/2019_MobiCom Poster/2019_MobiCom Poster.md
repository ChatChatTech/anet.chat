# ABSTRACT

One of the major challenges for fully enjoying the power of machine learning is the need for the high-quality labelled data. To tap-in the gold-mine of data generated by IoT devices with unprecedented volume and value, we discover and leverage the hidden connections among the multimodal data collected by various sensing devices. Different modal data can complete and learn from each other, but it is challenging to fuse multimodal data without knowing their perception (and thus the correct labels). In this work, we propose MultiSense, a paradigm for automatically mining potential perception, cross-labelling each modal data, and then improving the learning models over the set of multimodal data. We design innovative solutions for segmenting, aligning, and fusing multimodal data from different sensors. We implement our framework and conduct comprehensive evaluations on a rich set of data. Our results demonstrate that MultiSense significantly improves the data usability and the power of the learning models.

In practice, however, multi-modal data are severely underutilized because of lacking an effective way to process (e.g., segment and label) them. Facing highly diverse activities and events in real life, there is an urgent need to automatically segment and label multimodal sensing data and upgrade models for both unknown and known categories.

In this work we explore the hidden connections between multi-modal data for automatically cross-labelling and learning. We consider a typical sensing scenario where multimodal data are collected continuously from multiple objects by various sensing devices. Fig. 1(a) depicts such an example of a multi-person multimodal sensing. The inherent spatial and temporal correlations among multimodal data inspire us to propose a new paradigm, MultiSense which leverages results of some models to automatically label unrecognized data of other modalities, which is referred as cross-labelling. In this way, we turn unrecognized wasted data into valuable labelled data. Using these newly labelled data, we can further enhance each model to learn unknown activities from other models or improve its learning accuracy, which is referred to as cross-learning. Moreover, we can achieve more accurate results for activity recognition by fusing results of multimodal models. To achieve such an intelligent paradigm, we carefully design MultiSense to address the following critical challenges:

•Given unlabelled multimodal multi-person data streams, we design a set of techniques to segment, evaluate and align multimodal data. Specially, we propose a novel skeleton joints movement based method to transform a video into time sequences to facilitate segmentation and cross-modality alignment. A multi-scale segment-proposing algorithm is designed to accurately split multimodal data into segments of individual actions. Furthermore, a motion and motionless evidence-based method is designed to align multimodal data segments from the same person.

•Facing the fact that the initial capability of pre-trained models are limited and vary from each other, we design a strategy to fuse results of models of different modalities. It can deal with strongly conflicting predictions and produce more accurate data labels as well as recognition results.

# 3 IMPLEMENTATION & EVALUATION

# 3.1 Experiment Setup

We conduct the experiment in the indoor environment. Data are collected from 5 different types of sensors including a surveillance camera, microphones, smart watches, smartphones and Wi-Fi APs. We recruited 10 volunteers, including 7 males and 3 females. For each person, smart watch is worn on the right wrist while mobile phone is put in the left trousers pocket for collecting motion sensor data. In a singleperson scenario, each volunteer in the experiment room freely performed 9 types of activities(step, walk, bounce the ball, jump, knock, clap, punch, bow, and squat). In a multiperson scenario, two volunteers freely performed 9 types activities simultaneously in the same experiment room.

We extract features of multimodal segments using methods by [1–5]. For the classifier, we adopt SVM for all modalities. Each model uses the data from one of the sensors. In single-person scenarios, we set up four possible cases according to models initial abilities. Case 1: For each model we use data of all 9 activities for pre-training. Case 2: Only one model are trained with data of all 9 activities, and the rest models use data of a portion of activities. Case 3: For each model we use data of 5 randomly selected activities for pre-training. It is worth mentioning that activities of five models overlap. Case 4: Five models use data of disjoint activities for pre-training. For each activity category, we only use 10% data for pre-training and 90% for the cross-labelling and learning experiments.

# 3.2 Evaluation

Data-Person Alignment When there are multiple persons (e.g., two persons in our experiment) in the room, we first group multimodal data streams by person and discard segments which we cannot match with high confidence. Thanks to our design using both motion and motionless correlations among data streams, the alignment accuracy are higher than 90% in all four cases, even though models abilities are incomplete and even disjoint in Case 2 to 4.

Labelling accuracy improvement by aggregation. Fig.2 presents the labelling accuracy of each single model and the accuracy after aggregating predictions of different models (i.e., the cross-labelling accuracy). In all four cases, the accuracy after aggregation is better than the accuracy of every single model. The worse the initial ability of models, the more significant the improvement by aggregation. Specifically, in Case 1 and Case 2 the accuracy after aggregation is 98 5% and 91 8% respectively. In Case 3, although none of . .five models known all 9 activities, the accuracy after aggregation reaches 85 8%. In Case 4, the abilities of five models are disjoint, which is extremely challenging to combine the strongly conflicting predictions. Despite the poor performance of each single model, the accuracy can still achieves 67 9% after aggregation.

Model ability improvement. The ability of each model evolves as more data arrive. We use 3 disjoint data sets for 3 update iterations. In all four cases, the abilities of all models increase with the model updating. For example, in Case 1, all models can recognize 9 activities in the beginning. After only 3 rounds of model updating, accuracy of video model increases from 95.2% to 97.2%, accuracy of audio model increases from 91.5% to 92.1%, accuracy of smart watch model increases from 92.1% to 92.3%, accuracy of phone model increases from 71.2% to 75.2%, and accuracy of wireless model increases from 38.4% to 48.1%. In Case 2 to 4, models abilities are initially incomplete. After model updating, each model not only has a better overall accuracy but also learns all 9 activities since Round 2.
