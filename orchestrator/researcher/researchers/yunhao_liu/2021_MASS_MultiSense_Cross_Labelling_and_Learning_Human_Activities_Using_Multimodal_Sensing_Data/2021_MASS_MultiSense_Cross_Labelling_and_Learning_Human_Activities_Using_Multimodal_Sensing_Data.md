Lan Zhang, Daren Zheng, Zhengtao Wu, Mengjing Liu, Mu Yuan, Feng Han, Xiang-Yang Li
University of Science and Technology of China

zhanglan, xiangyangli@ustc.edu.cn, zdr123, wzt, lmj123, ym0813, hf1996@mail.ustc.edu.cn

Abstract—One of the major challenges for fully enjoying the power of machine learning is the need for the high-quality labelled data. To tap-in the gold-mine of data generated by IoT devices with unprecedented volume and value, we discover and leverage the hidden connections among the multimodal data collected by various sensing devices. Different modal data can complement and learn from each other, but it is challenging to fuse multimodal data without knowing their perception (and thus the correct labels). In this work, we propose MultiSense, a paradigm for automatically mining potential perception, cross labelling each modal data, and then improving the learning models for recognizing human activity accurately. We design innovative solutions for segmenting, aligning, and fusing multimodal data from different sensors. We implement our framework and conduct comprehensive evaluations on a rich set of data. Our results demonstrate that MultiSense significantly improves the data usability and the power of the learning models. With 9 diverse activities performed by users, our framework automatically labels multimodal sensing data generated by five different sensing mechanisms (video, smart watch, smartphone, audio, and wireless-channel) with an average accuracy 98.5%, while for each single-modal data model, the accuracy is 95.2%, 92.1%, 71.1%, 91.5%, and 34.8% respectively. Furthermore, it enables the existing models to learn unknown activities from other modalities and thus greatly improves the activity recognition accuracy.

# I. INTRODUCTION

The accelerated acquisition and widely deployment of multimodal sensing devices, e.g., smartphones, wearable devices, surveillance cameras and wireless access points, significantly increase the sensing capability of various human activities and events. In tandem with this increase, recently we have witnessed a dramatic rise in machine learning models. A great number of machine learning models are designed to understand the data of different modalities, including videos $[1]$ , $[2]$ , audio data $[3]$ , $[4]$ , $[5]$ , motion sensor data $[6]$ , $[7]$ , $[8]$ , and wireless signals $[9]$ , $[10]$ , $[11]$ . However, in practice there are several significant limitations restricting these intelligent sensing applications. Firstly, single-modal data only captures partial information of the whole scenario and often results in low understanding accuracy or even failure. For example, a video can only capture visual information of activities, while an audio can capture extra information implied by speech and sound. Moreover, different data streams may experience temporal or spatial capability diversities, e.g., video streams from different cameras often have different spatial coverage, and wireless signal or audio signal may not capture some activities due to long distances to subjects. Secondly, existing single-modal or multi-modal machine learning models are often designed for some specific task, and trained to classify data into limited predefined categories. Those models often fail to recognize and label data of unknown categories. Self-evolving machine learning systems are highly desired by applications in complex environments. Thirdly, to fully enjoy the power of existing sophisticated models, there is an urgent demand for large amounts of multimodal labelled data. Considering highly diverse activities and events in real life, however, manual annotation is time-consuming and labor-intensive. For some modality data (e.g., motion sensor data and wireless signal), it's quite difficult for both people and models to label. Though many exiting works utilize labelled multimodal data samples, few works address how to segment and label multimodal data streams.

To address these problems, taking advantages of the intrinsic correlation among multimodal data, we came up with the idea of leveraging recognition results of some modalities to automatically label unrecognized data of other modalities (referred to as cross-labelling and then helping existing models evolve to learn unknown categories as well as to achieve better recognition accuracy (referred to as cross-learning). In this work, we focus on a typical sensing scenario where multimodal data are collected continuously for multiple objects by various sensing devices, e.g., smart home and smart healthcare. To achieve the vision of cross-labelling and cross-learning, we need to address following critical challenges:

Challenge 1. How to align multimodal data streams from the same person? An intuitive idea is to use human and device identification information, e.g., device ID for wearable devices, and face recognition by CV-based methods. However, it may be unavailable due to privacy or authority issue. Even with this information, we need pre-establish the correspondence between people and devices, which may be infeasible in open environment. Thus we prefer to design a general alignment method with only unlabelled multimodal multi-person data.

Challenge 2. How to correctly segment continuous streams into actions automatically? Segmenting data streams by activity is a critical operation for data labelling and learning [12]. However, there is a “segment and prediction” paradox: for better segmentation of data stream, we need to recognize the activity described by each segment; for better activity recognition, we need partition the data such that each segment only contains the data for one activity, no more no less.

Challenge 3. How to solve strongly conflicting outputs of machine learning models of different modalities and determine proper labels for unrecognized segments? Pre-trained models of different modalities may provide strongly conflicting predictions for the same activity. The conflicts could come from two sources: the incompetent initial capabilities of machine learning models, and the different spatial-coverage and sensing capabilities of different sensing devices. We need carefully address the conflicts to improve prediction and labelling.

Facing these challenges, we propose, design, and implement a novel paradigm MultiSense. Our contributions are as follows.

\- We introduce the notions of cross-labelling and cross-learning and design the first such paradigm MultiSense that automatically turns massive unrecognized multimodal data into valuable labelled data, as well as empowers multimodal activity recognition systems to achieve self-evolution.

• To validate the efficacy and efficiency of our approaches, we conduct comprehensive experiments by recruiting 10 volunteers to conduct 9 diverse activities with a time-span of a month and collect multimodal dataset generated by five different sensing mechanisms (video, smart watch, smartphone, audio, and wireless-channel). Our framework automatically labels these five multimodal sensing data streams with an average accuracy 98.5%, while for each conventional single-modal data model, the accuracy is 95.1% (video), 91.8% (smart watch), 71.1% (smartphone), 91.2% (audio), and 34.4% (wireless) respectively. Furthermore, it enables the existing models to learn unknown activities from other modalities and thus greatly improves the activity recognition accuracy by retraining the models using newly labelled data.

# II. RELATED WORK

Multimodal applications and models. Massive multimodal data are being generated by various devices in a broad range of application scenarios. The benefit from multimodal data is that we can get diversity view and complementary information compared with unimodal data. Also due to sensing system heterogeneity, the performance of unimodal modal may have sharply lower classification accuracy in unconstrained and real-world conditions while multimodal modal can alleviate this situation [13]. In addition when one or several modality data is missing, multimodal models can still operate [14]. A series of efforts have been devoted to utilizing multimodal data to achieve better understanding of scenarios and enhance the robustness or security of models, e.g.. video analysis [15], [16], [17], web mining [18], [19], emotion recognition [20], [21], human activity recognition [22], [23], [24], and mobile positioning [25]. Those works mainly focus on how to embed heterogeneous multimodal data with higher discrimination to achieve better prediction accuracy. Typically, for spatial temporal sensing data, Supreme [26] proposed a spatial-temporal reconstruction network to solve how to represent complex spatial-temporal correlations and external factors with spatial-temporal residual block and external factor fusion module in fine-grained radio map reconstruction problem. DeepFusion [23] presented a unified multi-sensor deep learning framework, which can combine different sensors' information weighted by the quality of their data and incorporate cross-sensor correlations to improve the sensing performance. GlobalFusion [24] proposed a global attention mechanism for applying deep learning techniques to IoT application. Global attention can correlate global context derived from higher level features of neural network with local sensor features to find informative ones and filter out the unrelated noise, which is different from previous attention mechanisms. Most of those work are supervised, thus requiring well aligned and labelled multimodal data samples. One of the major challenges for the wide adoption of those models is how to efficiently and accurately align and label multimodal data.

Multimodal data annotation. Manually labelling data is time-consuming and difficult, especially for multimodal data streams from multiple objects. One way to tackle this problem is to design models to make full use of limited labelled data or noisy labelled data. Huang et al. [27] propose an annotation framework for learning multimodal joint embedding space. The real-world datasets may be noisy, resulting in not ideal classification accuracy. SGSF (Small groups based serial fusion) [28] is proposed for removing low-density noise samples whilst keeping the high-density outliers samples without destroying the data distribution. Semi-supervised learning (SSL) is a widely adopted methodology for generating pseudo-labels. Most existing SSL models only consider a single modality [29], [30], [31]. Recently, a few works are tailored for some specific multimodal scenarios. For example, a deep generative model by semi-supervised learning is proposed for incomplete multimodal emotional data [21]. Another way is to minimize data annotation cost by using active learning, however, most of which focus on single-modal scenarios, e.g., [6], [32]. There are also some works addressing knowledge transferring across modalities. RecycleML [33] proposes a cross-modal training approach, which requires manually specifying the objective of transferring. All those works assume that multimodal data streams of the same object have already been correctly aligned and segmented, which however are nontrivial in complex environments.

In this work, we address the multimodal data auto-labelling and model auto-updating issues in more general and complex scenarios (e.g., smart home and smart healthcare) where multimodal data are collected continuously for multiple objects by various sensing devices, and the initial ability of models of different modalities could vary widely. Our proposed segmentation, alignment and labelling techniques can work as the pre-operations or complementary of existing multimodal models, and our paradigm can also utilize existing models in a black-box manner.

# III. PROBLEM FORMULATION & DESIGN OVERVIEW

# A. Concept and Problem Definition

We explore a typical sensing scenario, e.g., Fig. 1, where various sensing devices collect multimodal data for multiple persons. Co-located sensors may capture different features of the same activity and produce highly correlated multimodal data. Inspired by this insight, we propose a new sensing data labelling and learning paradigm, MultiSense, which leverages the sensing power of multimodal data and recognition power of multiple learning models to enable automatic data labelling (i.e., cross-labelling) and improve model capability (i.e., cross-learning). Moreover, better recognition accuracy for each activity can be achieved by fusing results of multiple models.

Fig. 1: An example of a multi-person multimodal sensing scenario and the overview of the system. In this example scenario, there are three people typing, bouncing the ball and walking respectively. In this A variety of sensing devices, including cameras, microphones, wireless APs, smart watches and smartphones, are collecting data streams of different modalities.

Formally, let $A = \{a_{1}, \cdots, a_{N}\}$ be the universe of activities, which contains N activities under study. There are M modalities of sensing data. In this work, we consider four modal sensing data: 1) video captured by surveillance cameras; 2) motion sensor data collected by smart watches or smartphones; 3) audio data captured by microphones of smart watches or surveillance cameras; 4) wireless signals captured by APs. During initialization, for each modality there exist a small set of labelled data $T_{i}$ for a subset of activities $A_{i} \subseteq A$ . Here i is the index for the i-th data modality. Using data set $T_{i}$ as the training data, a model (or a set of models) $M_{i}$ can be trained to recognize activities in $A_{i}$ with a certain degree of accuracy, which indicates the initial capability of the learning model. When the system is working, data streams of different modalities arrive continuously, which contain information of various activities performed by multiple persons. Let $D_{i}^{j}$ be the j-th data stream of the i-th modality.

The objective of MultiSense is threefold: 1) partition each data stream $D_{i}^{j}$ to segments $D_{i}^{j}[t_{s}, t_{e}]$ so that each segment contains only information for one action, e.g., one walking step or one keystroke. Here $t_{s}$ and $t_{e}$ are the start and end time of this data segment; 2) labelling those data segments with high accuracy; 3) updating existing models using newly labelled data to enhance ability of these machine learning models. The first two objectives are to produce high-quality multimodal labelled data in an automatic and continuous way. The third objective is to achieve self-evolution for the multimodal activity recognition system. The main challenges and opportunity both arise from the fact that the initial ability of models of different modalities could vary widely.

# B. System Architecture

The overview of MultiSense is presented in Fig. 1, which includes the following main components.

![](images/5a534aebe85a6859ba1aa45f1e67ff981c99896fa855b7ccae9f2853d42f9bb9.jpg)



Fig. 2: Example of transforming video to time sequences.

1. Preprocessing. We first split the complex data streams which contain activity information of multiple persons to multiple data streams. As a result, each data stream only contains information of one person. Then we transform all data streams to a unified form–time sequences and use a band-pass filter to eliminate noises. Specially, we propose to transform video to time sequences of data points to facilitate the exploration of connection between a video stream and sequences of other modalities. It requires the sequences to capture key movement information of activities. We notice that wearable devices usually are attached or close to body joints. Inspired by this insight, we extract 2D skeletons of people in a video [34], [35], [36] and track the movement of joints to generate a time sequence for each person, as shown in Fig. 2.

2. Multimodal segment proposing. We need partition each time sequences data into segments so that each segment reflects a complete action, e.g. one step and one keystroke. Facing the raw data without any pre-knowledge of user's activity, we design a multi-scale segment proposing method to output a set of segment hypotheses with high confidence. Extracting feature vectors of all segment hypotheses, we predict their labels (with confidence values) using pre-trained machine learning models.   
3. Label evaluation. Given a machine learning model, a high-confidence prediction doesn't necessarily mean a high accuracy. For proposed segments and their predicted labels, we evaluate the reliability of those labels by analyzing their distribution in the feature space.   
4. Data-person alignment. In a multi-person scenario, before cross-modality labelling, we need to determine which group of data sequences describe activities by the same person. With only incomplete data segments and unreliable predictions,

it is difficult to align a bunch of data sequences to their corresponding person correctly. Especially when the models of different modalities have disjoint ability (output disjoint labels), their predictions do not provide any help to grouping multimodal data. To deal with this challenge, we use not only motion evidence but also motionless evidence to achieve high-accuracy cross-modality data-person alignment.

5. Prediction aggregation and cross-labelling. Obtaining multimodal data segments of the same person, we design a prediction aggregation mechanism which combines evidences (predictions) of different modalities to make the final decision of the person's activities. The reliability of each prediction (computed in Step 3) is also taken into consideration. Compared with existing evidence combination rule, our mechanism can deal with strongly conflicting evidences when models' ability vary greatly. We select segments successfully proposed in the Step 2 and label them.   
6. Model updating. With newly labelled data segments, we can use them as new training samples to iteratively update existing model to learn unknown activities or increase the recognition accuracy of known activities.

As shown in Fig. 1, newly labelled data can be further used to improve the performance of segment proposing and updated models can be adopted to provide more and better predictions in the next iteration. In this way, MultiSense is a closed-loop sensing system which continues evolving into a more intelligent one. Consequently, our system provides system extensibility where a user can input labelled data (of known categories or unknown categories) from other sources in any iteration. With our design, newly collected labelled data of any modality will empower not only model for this modality but also learning models for other modalities to recognize more activities of the input data.

# IV. CROSS LABELLING AND LEARNING

As the data arrives continuously, by preprocessing, we transform each data stream $D_{i}^{k}$ into a time sequence $S_{i}^{k}$ . Here $D_{i}^{k}$ is the k-th data stream of the i-th modality. In an uncontrolled environment, people can perform activities freely. As a result, each time sequence may contain multiple diverse activities. Our goal is to process each time sequence $S_{i}^{k}$ to obtain a set of labelled segments $S_{i}^{k} = \{S_{i}^{k}[1], S_{i}^{k}[2], \cdots\}$ , where $S_{i}^{k}[n] = (\mathbb{S}_{i}^{k}[t_{s}^{n} : t_{e}^{n}], L_{i}^{k}[n])$ . Here $t_{s}^{n}$ and $t_{e}^{n}$ are the start time and end time for the n-th segment $S_{i}^{k}[n]$ . Each segment should only contain a single action, thus segments from the same sequence should not overlap temporally. $L_{i}^{k}[n] \in A$ is the label of this segment, which is determined by MultiSense and should be as accurate as possible. This task is nontrivial due to the 'segment and prediction' paradox: we need to segment data sequence by activity before we feed the data into a model to predict activities. Moreover, the initial ability of pre-trained models are limited and vary from one another. They may provide highly conflicting predictions for data of the same activity. In this section, we solve the segmentation, alignment, prediction fusing and cross-labelling issues in the following subsections.

# A. Multimodal Segment Proposal

Given a time sequence, an activity can be at any position and last for any duration. To cope with both diversity of different activities and diversity of the same activity performed by different persons, the basic idea of segment proposing is using the historical labelled data segments as templates to conduct multi-scale search on the newly arrived time sequences. Those segments matching one of the templates will be proposed as candidate segments. For those segments not being successfully proposed by existing templates, most of which are segments of unknown activities, will be re-selected and labelled according to their correlated segments in other modalities (in Section IV-D). Specially, we don't segment audio data using this template proposing way. After filtering, the audio data consists of a number of very short waves (about 100 ms) and long intervals among them. Thus we can easily divide an audio time sequence into segments by detecting those intervals.

For each modality, we already have a small set of labelled data segments $T_{i}$ for a subset of activity $A_{i} \subseteq A$ . For each activity $a_{j} \in A_{i}$ , we select one data segment as the template $T_{i}^{j} \in T_{i}$ of this activity category. Here the template $T_{i}^{j}$ is the centroid of segments for the activity $a_{j}$ , that is, it has the smallest average DTW distance to all other segments labelled with $a_{j}$ . Given a sequence $S_{i}^{k}$ , we use every template $T_{i}^{j}$ as a sliding window to search the potential segment with minimum DTW distance to the template at multiple scales for each local range. The DTW distances at multiple scales are calculated to deal with the diversity of different persons. Then we input all potential segments into the pre-trained model $M_{i}$ to obtain their labels and corresponding confidences. If the predicted label of a segment is inconsistent with the template label, then this proposal is not reliable. Thus, we remove such inconsistent segments from this phase and re-select and label them during the prediction aggregation phase. The remaining segments are proposed by templates of different activities, thus there could be conflicting proposals (i.e., temporally overlapping segments). For each pair of temporally overlapping segments, we reserve the one whose label has a higher confidence. The detailed method is presented in Algorithm 1.

# B. Label Evaluation

With Algorithm 1, we obtain a set of segments for each time sequence, which compose only a portion of the time sequence, and their corresponding prediction results produced by pretrained models. For each data segment $S_{i}^{k}[n]$ , its prediction result is a set $M_{i}(S_{i}^{k}[n]) = \{(a_{j}, c(a_{j})) | a_{j} \in \mathbb{A}_{i}\}$ . $c(a_{j})$ is the confidence for the predicted activity category $a_{j}$ , and $\sum_{a_{j} \in \mathbb{A}_{i}} c(a_{j}) = 1$ . However, a predicted label with a high confidence doesn't necessarily mean it is reliable because the ability of the model may be quite limited at the beginning (i.e., $|\mathbb{A}_i|$ is much smaller than $|\mathbb{A}|$ ). Due to the ignorance of the model, the model could output a wrong label for a data segment of an unknown activity with a high confidence. We would like to quantify the reliability of each prediction and give every unreliable evidence a discount when we conduct alignment and aggregation. Based on an intuitive observation that feature vectors of data segments belonging to the same category should be close to each other in the feature space, the discount for a prediction $(a_{j}, c(a_{j}))$ is measured by the distances between the feature vector of $S_{i}^{k}[n]$ and feature vectors of other historical data segments belonging to the activity $a_{j}$ . The discount is

$$
\operatorname{Disc} \left(S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ], a _ {j}\right) = - \lambda \frac {\operatorname{AvgDis} \left(F _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ] , a _ {j}\right) - \operatorname{AvgDis} \left(a _ {j}\right)}{\operatorname{std} \left(a _ {j}\right)}. \tag {1}
$$

Here $F_{i}^{k}[n]$ is the feature vector of $S_{i}^{k}[n]$ . $AvgDis(F_{i}^{k}[n], a_{j})$ is the average euclidean distance of $F_{i}^{k}[n]$ to historical data segments in the class $a_{j}$ . $AvgDis(a_{j})$ is the average euclidean distance within the class $a_{j}$ , and $std(a_{j})$ is the standard deviation of pair-wise feature distances in the class $a_{j}$ . $\lambda$ is a parameter for adjusting the influence of the discount.

# C. Data-Person Alignment

When there are N > 1 persons, it is necessary to conduct data-person alignment to group multimodal data sequences of the same person together to ensure correct cross-modality labelling. We propose to use both motion and motionless correlations for grouping.

1) Motion correlation: Generally, multimodal data sequences of the same person should have more consistent activity labels (in both label category and time) than that of different persons. As a result, we can compute the cross-correlations of labels among time sequences to find the optimal N matching groups to maximize the average intra-group cross-correlation. In some cases, however, the initial ability of models could be very limited, which results in highly unreliable predicted labels. Especially, when the ability of models are totally different, they output disjoint labels for data sequences of different modalities, which provide no clue to assign sequences to different groups. To tackle this problem, we leverage a very special activity “Motionless”, which can be easily recognized for all data modalities as a cross-modality evidence.

2) Motionless correlation: Motionless segments (e.g., standing or sitting still) of different data sequences can be simply detected using amplitude thresholds. Then, multimodal data sequences of the same person should have temporally consistent motionless segments. We find that the motionless correlation is much more reliable than the motion correlation. So we group data sequences according to motionless correlation first and adopt motion correlation only when multiple persons keep moving without any noticeable pause or they pause and restart moving simultaneously. In a corner case where multiple persons perform exactly the same activity synchronously, activity labels of different persons' data sequences are identical. Then we cannot distinguish data of different persons, however, a wrong grouping won't cause incorrect cross-modality labelling results. In another rare corner case where multiple persons perform different activities synchronously and unfortunately the output labels for multimodal data sequences are disjoint, we discard those data sequences to guarantee the accuracy of cross-labelling.

# D. Prediction Aggregation and Cross-labelling

Now we obtain groups of multimodal data segments. Each group contains data of an individual. Each segment $S_{i}^{k}[n]$ has a prediction result $M_{i}(S_{i}^{k}[n]) = \{(a_{j}, c(a_{j})) | a_{j} \in \mathbb{A}_{i}\}$ with its corresponding discount values $\{Disc(S_{i}^{k}[n], a_{j})\}$ . For concurrent segments of different modalities, their predictions may be conflicting. For example, at the same moment, the label of the smart watch data is “running” while the label of the wireless signal is “fighting”. Thus, to achieve accurate activity recognition and cross-labelling, we need to aggregate conflicting predictions of different models and make a final decision for each data segment.

Here, we cannot directly adopt the traditional D-S theory (Dempster-Shafer evidence theory) [37] to fuse predictions. The combination rule of traditional D-S theory is ineffective to deal with strongly conflicting evidences when multiple models have quite different abilities. For example, a model may not recognize the activity “fighting”, thus won’t output a “fighting” label, which means it gives zero confidence for this category. Since the Dempster rule of combination is based on conjunctive operation (AND), “fighting” will never be selected as the final decision no matter what are the outputs of other models. To solve this combining problem facing highly conflicting evidences, we design a mechanism working as follows:

(1) Given the prediction result $\{(a_j, c(a_j)) | a_j \in \mathbb{A}_i\}$ of $S_i^k[n]$ , for each label $a_j$ , instead of defining $m(a_j) = c(a_j)$ we define the mass function as $m(\{a_j\} \cup (\mathbb{A} \setminus \mathbb{A}_i)) = c(a_j)$ . Then $m(\mathbb{A}_i \setminus \{a_j\}) = 1 - c(a_j)$ . Here $\mathbb{A} \setminus \mathbb{A}_i$ represents the ignorance of the model $M_i$ . This mass function reflects that, when a model $M_i$ produces a label $a_j$ for a data segment, with a high probability the true activity is either $a_j$ or an unknown activity for $M_i$ .

(2) Since the reliability of predictions varies with the ability of models, we reduce the influence of an unreliable prediction by discounting its confidence as

$$
S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ]. c (a _ {j}) = S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ]. c (a _ {j}) \exp (D i s c (S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ], a _ {j})). \qquad (2)
$$

(3) With adjusted confidences we can calculate mass functions for all predictions, each of which is an evidence. For each set of concurrent multimodal segments, we combine their evidences to compute the belief for each decision $a_{j} \in \mathbb{A}$ . The belief is the probability that $a_{j}$ is supported by evidences, which is computed as

$$
\begin{array}{l} B e l \left(\left\{a _ {j} \right\}\right) = \frac {1}{K} \sum_ {\cap \mathbb {A} _ {i j} = \left\{a _ {j} \right\}} \prod_ {1 \leq i \leq M, 1 \leq j \leq \left| \mathbb {A} _ {i} \right|} m \left(\mathbb {A} _ {i j}\right), \tag {3} \\ \text { where } K = 1 - \sum_ {\bigcap A _ {i j} = \emptyset} \prod_ {1 \leq i \leq M, 1 \leq j \leq | \mathbb {A} _ {i} |} \\ \end{array}
$$

We choose the decision with highest belief as the final prediction of all data segments in this concurrent set.

Using this mechanism, we determine a label for each set of concurrent multimodal segments. Note that, not every set contains segments of all modalities. For some set, during its time interval there are some modalities for which no segments have been successfully proposed. Now, we can split and label segments of those modalities according to the time interval and prediction of the labelled set.

# E. Model Updating

As MultiSense runs, newly labelled multimodal data segments are produced by cross-labelling continuously. A machine learning system can take those fresh data segments as training samples to iteratively update existing models, making them learn unknown activities or increase the accuracy of known activities. There are two principles for updating models. First, the updated models' prediction accuracy for known activities should not deteriorate greatly; Second, the prediction accuracy for new activity categories should be as high as possible. Obeying these principles, if the prediction accuracy drops beyond an acceptable threshold, the system can rollback the model and discard the data collected in this iteration. With the newly labelled segments, various model updating mechanisms, e.g., retraining and finetuning, can be adopted by MultiSense. The design of model updating mechanisms is out of scope of this work.

# V. IMPLEMENTATION & EVALUATION

# A. Experiment Setup

We conducted experiment in the indoor environment. Data were collected from 5 different types of sensors including a surveillance camera, microphones, smart watches, smartphones and Wi-Fi APs. We recruited 10 volunteers (7 males and 3 females). Each volunteer wore a smart watch on his/her right wrist and put a mobile phone in his/her left trousers pocket. They performed 9 types of activities (as shown in Table I). We study two cases: 1) In a single-person scenario, each volunteer freely performed 9 types of activities in the experiment room; 2) In a multi-person scenario, two volunteers freely performed 9 types activities simultaneously in the same room. In both cases, five types of devices collected sensing data continuously. Totally we collected over 1000 minutes data.

For each modality, we extract features of segments using corresponding popular methods, including Openpose $[34]$ , $[35]$ , $[36]$ , MFCC, Lasagna $[8]$ , and Motion-Fi $[9]$ . For the classifier, we adopt SVM for all modalities to show the baseline performance of our design. Both the feature extraction models and classifiers can be replaced with more advanced ones in the future, which can yield even better performance. Each model uses the data from one type of the sensors. For each activity category, we only use 10% data as training data and 90% for the cross-labelling and learning experiments. In single-person scenarios, we set up four possible cases with different initial abilities of models. Case 1: For each model we use training data of all 9 activities for pre-training. # VI. CONCLUSION

In this work, we presented MultiSense for automatically labelling multi-modal data collected by a variety of sensors monitoring certain objects. It turns a large set of unlabelled data into gold-mine for machine learning models and improves the prediction accuracy of these learning models using cross-learning and fusion. Our comprehensive experiments demonstrated the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed framework. There are still several challenging tasks left for fully maximizing the advantages of this framework. The first challenge is to improve the robustness and efficiency for aligning the data from multiple objects in more complex scenarios. The second is to further improve the labelling and learning accuracy, especially for the audio data and the backscattered wireless signal data. The third is to reduce the cost of data preprocessing.
