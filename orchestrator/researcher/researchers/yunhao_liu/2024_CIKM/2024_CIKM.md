# Abstract

As the mobile gaming market experiences significant growth, there is a continuous emergence of new gaming products such as premium games and video mini-games. Meeting their marketing needs and supporting their business growth is essential for long-term prosperity. However, compared with extensive studies on user modeling such as LTV prediction, much less attention has been drawn to special gaming products, especially in terms of understanding their lifecycle stages and corresponding demands. Unlike modeling individual users, understanding games is closely tied to user behavior: the lifecycle of a game encompasses the entire process from initial user interaction to churn, and by accurately identifying and tracking the evolution of the game lifecycle can lead to better personal service. This raises the necessity of comprehensively understanding the lifecycle process model of the game. In this paper, we introduce the GameTrail - Probabilistic Lifecycle Process Model, designed to construct the complete lifecycle and stage representation for games and users through long-term repeated interactions. Specifically, we first initiate the complete game lifecycle using a joint probabilistic stochastic process model by defining the lifecycle stages of both

# 1 Introduction

In the rapidly evolving gaming industry, a wide variety of highquality games and mini-games are continuously emerging. At the same time, an increasing number of game developers are utilizing advertising platforms to advertise their game products. Although game products play an important role in this market, there is little attention on understanding the games themselves in both academia and industry. Existing research mainly concentrates on user behavior and preferences. Understanding the needs of both games and users can help companies formulate more effective marketing strategies, provide more personalized services, and improve product design and operations. Therefore, many companies are committed to gaining in-depth insights into games and users, leveraging this information to optimize their business operations.

Recently, there have been many research work related to user lifecycle model[10, 15, 16, 18, 25, 27, 31]. In the gaming industry, the user lifecycle model describes the entire process from the user’s initial registration with a game to churn, which could be divided into five stages: registration, growth, maturity, dormancy, and churn. Typically, as the product lifecycle theory[3, 14], games also have their lifecycle stages, which include introduction, growth, stability, and decline. For example, games in the decline phase might focus on controlling costs, while new games should strive for increased exposure opportunities. We study and construct a long-term interaction process dataset - Industrial Gaming Datasets, from a real online gaming Ads platform, which consists of numerous interaction sequences among < ????????, ????????, ?????? >(see Section 5 for dataset details). In Fig. 1 left, we consider the long-term interaction process of user ?? at game ?? as a local game-user-specific lifecycle process, where each interaction process contains a long-term user behavior sequence(circles) on a game - click, purchase, active, and so on, which reflects the dynamic nature of user interests and game stages. We aggregate these local lifecycles from all users ?? ∈ U of game ??, we can obtain a global lifecycle process for the game ?? as in Fig. 1 right. In Fig. 1 right, we present the trends in user activity levels for games in the periodic update stage, the initial growth stage, and the stability stage. As the game is in a different lifecycle stage, the exhibited game’s global states vary, along with the local lifecycles of each user. Thus, in this work, we aim to design a comprehensive lifecycle process model and stage representation for diverse games and users, capturing the dynamics of in-game stage transitions and user interest changes.

Compared to modeling user aspects, accurately understanding the current stage and practical needs of games from a full-lifecycle process fashion is more challenging for several reasons. (1) Dynamic Nature of Gaming Industry. The gaming industry evolves rapidly, with new trends, technologies, and user preferences constantly emerging. This dynamic environment makes it challenging to keep up with the evolving needs of games. Specifically, in the gaming industry, games typically have various irregular promotions, such as version updates, anniversary celebrations, gameplay updates, and server mergers. (2) Domain knowledge dependence. From Fig. 1 left, the interaction between each game and each user is a long-term repeated process, which is also volatile and highly dynamic across different games. These prior works consider the user’s lifecycle stages where interaction with each item occurs only once[15, 31] via explicit stage definition, such as New, Wander, Stick, and Loyal in STAN[15], which relies on domain-specific knowledge to ensure its availability. When faced with a large number of games and diverse advertising contexts, it’s impossible to find a unified stage definition. (3) Limited Data Availability. Unlike user data, which can be collected through various analytics tools and platforms, obtaining comprehensive data on the state and demands of games can be more challenging due to limitations in availability, while the game’s state is more sparse and diverse.

To address the above issues, we propose GameTrail - Probabilistic Lifecycle Process Model in this paper. Our model can be summarized Game Lifecycle Process in three major aspects: constructing the local game-user-specific lifecycle process model, inferencing the global latent game lifecycle stage, and capturing the dynamic in-game stage transitions. (1) Lifecycle Model by Probabilistic Stochastic Process. To better understand games’ complete lifecycle, we consider each long-term repeated interaction as a stochastic process, which encompasses users’ interest or preference for games and the impact of games’ lifecycle on users. We construct a probabilistic stochastic process model to describe the long-term repeated process while defining users’ interest or preference for games and the impact of games’ lifecycle on users as latent stochastic variables. This approach involves analyzing the evolution process of a game from its introduction to its decline, considering the uncertainties and randomness inherent in market dynamics. (2) Interpretable Stage Representation. To acquire latent probabilistic parameters, we suggest employing variational inference (VI)[13]. In our framework, the game’s complete lifecycle is reflected through its interactive history with all users, which is defined as game-specific parameters that capture impacts that are common across all sequences regardless of the associated game and user. By contrast, the latent stochastic variables we have defined above describe game-user-specific processes, which have to be inferred from fewer, game-user-specific data, motivating a Bayesian treatment by the VI[13]. (3) Capture In-game Stage Transitions. We first approach the impact of lifecycle stage transitions on target prediction from the perspective of probabilistic KL divergence. Subsequently, we consider the influence of the more recent advertising context changes among in-game stage transitions using online learning and cross-attention mechanisms. Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

• To the best of our knowledge, it is the first time that the game-user lifecycle process models and unified stage representations are jointly learned via a deep probabilistic model to enhance downstream target prediction.   
• Our model not only extracts suitable lifecycle embeddings for diverse games and users from the long-term repeated interaction data but also dynamically adapts to the changes

in advertising context due to the stage transitions within games.

• Extensive offline experiments and online downstream target prediction on a live gaming industry dataset have shown the effectiveness of 48% and 17% on NMSE and NMAE by comparing several competitive baselines.

# 2 Related Work

User Representation for LTV Prediction. The existing work primarily focuses on user understanding and representation, utilizing user attribute information and historical behavioral data within a specific game (category) to build advanced prediction models for better forecasting user relative behavior. Specifically on customer lifetime value(CLV/LTV), conventional approaches such as RFM[7, 11, 21, 29], machine learning[2, 6, 19, 20], and deep learning[22, 30] have been utilized. MDLUR[28] proposes a universal user embedding architecture by exploring multiple data sources for downstream tasks, specifically LTV prediction. TSUR[23] utilizes historical revenue time series and user attributes to learn both temporal user representations via Wavelet Transform with Multi-headed attention and structural user representations by using Graph Attention Network. MSDP[9] proposes robust user behavioral sequence representation by learning the user’s behavior distribution over a period of time as the self-supervision signal. These approaches in the above work mainly focus on how to integrate multi-source user datasets and employ suitable analytical algorithms to understand users. Currently, there is also some work understanding users in the gaming industry, including M3Rec[5], and SCGRec[26]. Moreover, these efforts predominantly focus on recommendation systems and associated downstream tasks, thereby leading to more targeted and effective In-App Purchase (IAP) strategies and In-App Advertisement (IAA) strategies.

Lifecycle-based Representation Model. With the needs of business management, an increasing number of personalized product and user lifecycle models are being proposed. Some studies construct user full-lifecycle models to identify long-term behavioral patterns, such as [16, 18, 25, 27]. LURM[25] proposes a novel framework called a full-lifecycle user representation model to construct general-purpose user representation research on extremely long behavior sequences since user registration. Nonetheless, these models often capture long-term user behaviors coarsely, neglecting distinctions between different lifecycle stages. In another segment of the research, distinct definitions for user lifecycle stages are initially proposed based on specific scenarios and then provide learning and representation algorithms for these stages[10, 15, 31]. Product life theory[3, 14] proposes four distinct stages including Introduction, Growth, Maturity and Decline. [10] uses a mathematical tool to model the product life stages and predict the transit probability between different stages. STAN[15] first proposes New, Wander, Stick, and Loyal as the definitions for user lifecycle stages in the e-commerce platform, and learns user preferences for Multi-Task Recommendation by utilizing user behaviors and then represents latent user stages based on these learned preferences. SASNet[31] defines user lifecycle stages in the context of a travel recommendation platform as Exploring, Planning, Pre-trip, and On-trip, and proposes a novel stage-aware deep sequential matching network to capture the evolving of user stages and perform deep user-item matching for travel-related recommendation.

The explicitly defined approach is not sensitive enough to capture the transitions of user lifecycle stages. Therefore, various earlier studies have made attempts to delve into this insight using reinforcement learning[12, 24]. They utilize the online feedback mechanism of reinforcement learning, defining the lifecycle stage as a state module and the downstream prediction task as an action module. Based on a predefined reward function, after each action is executed, they collect the updated stage data and retrain the action function. [12] consider the product life dynamics, in a continuous, numerical manner and pay attention to the cold-start recommendation where items in the earlier stages $( \mathrm { i . e . } ,$ , introduction and growth) by proposing a novel RL-LTV framework for optimizing the longerterm rewards of items, the LTVs. However, such solutions need to consider the practical business issues arising from the explorationexploitation trade-off in reinforcement learning algorithms. On the other hand, companies may not want to update action functions based on large datasets frequently.

Our work builds upon these existing studies, but we are the first to propose dynamic adaptive analysis for both games’ and users’ lifecycle, to enhance downstream prediction tasks such as game activity and user spending. We utilize probabilistic stochastic process models to characterize and learn the lifecycle stage representations and take into account online learning and inference of downstream tasks based on the in-game lifecycle stage transitions.

# 4 Methods

In this section, we propose our GameTrail to represent the lifecycle process and capture the stage transition for modeling long-term repeated interaction datasets. Our core idea is to construct common prior distributions for game-specific and user-specific latent lifecycle stages, respectively, and to learn the game-user-specific latent stage variables based on long-term repeated interaction history among each game and user via probabilistic stochastic process models, and then aggregate the common game-specific and userspecific stage representations by the mixture of experts. These representations will be used as inputs for downstream prediction tasks. Meanwhile, we will also consider the latent stage transitions and online advertising context embeddings to obtain stationarity predictions.

$$

Considering the non-stationarity of data in ads optimization, we observe that online learning and selective advertising context embeddings demonstrate better performance in practical applications[17].

# 5 Experiments

In this section, we perform extensive offline and online experiments using both a public gaming dataset and a real-world gaming industry dataset from an online gaming Ads platform to evaluate the effectiveness of our proposed model GameTrail. Our experiments are designed to answer the following research questions:

RQ1. How does the performance of our proposed method compare with other state-of-the-art methods?

RQ2. How do the Latent Encoder module and Dynamic Encoder module impact the prediction results?

RQ3. Can the proposed lifecycle process model effectively represent the latent stages?

# 5.1 Experimental Setup

5.1.1 Industrial Gaming Datasets. The industrial gaming dataset used in this paper is generated by some currently active games and a large number of game players from a real-world online gaming Ads platform, as shown in Table 1. It comprises 90 active game datasets between 2022.01.01 and 2023.12.31. User attribute data primarily includes attribute data related to the individual users, such as gender, age, city level, and occupation. The game attribute data primarily represents the characteristics of the game itself, such as game ID, game name, game type, game core, game theme, game subtheme, market type, game battle type, and profit mode. The industrial dataset also includes the daily interaction behaviors of users and games between 2022.01.01 and 2023.12.31, including login time, active gameplay duration, the amount spent, and some in-game behaviors, presented in a time-series or sequence format, shown in Figure 1. Besides, there are the in-game advertising context and budget datasets to make us capture the in-game lifecycle transitions. All the datasets presented in this paper were utilized with the games’ and users’ explicit consent to the Privacy Policy.

5.1.2 Public Gaming Datasets. The public game dataset was collected from a publicly available game data repository website1. We selected various types of games, including large-scale premium games, app games, and video mini-games, and extracted data such as historical user gameplay records, ratings, prices, and sales figures for each game.

5.1.3 Baseline. We first introduce comparative methods from three categories as follows. We utilized openly available libraries and code obtained from GitHub, as referenced or provided in the original paper, to replicate these baseline models.

Classifical statistical model. RFM[7, 8, 11, 21, 29] conduct a classical statistical model via three parametric models with different distribution assumptions to describe Recency, Frequency, Monetary value of the repeated interaction. However, the RFM model cannot leverage games’ and users’ static attributes and portrait data and primarily focuses on estimating recency and frequency value. For Monetary value, we adopt a multiplier heuristic estimation approach[1], which proposes a simple, intuitive managerial heuristic: multiply the revenue observed in the previous ?? days by a constant as an estimate for the future.

To facilitate comparison, all metrics were consistently reported to 4 decimal places.

5.1.5 Experiment Settings. For the Industrial Gaming Datasets, we first use the prediction of users’ daily staytime and spending, games’ Daily Active Users (DAU), and monthly Active Users (MAU), as downstream tasks, conducting comparative experiments and validation experiments based on various methods. We conducted a comparison between our method and these baseline methods, utilizing the long-term repeated interaction datasets from the previous ?? = 30 days to predict the user and game activity outcomes, such as DAU/MAU/Payment in the future $\Delta = [ 1 , 7 , 3 0 ]$ days. By leveraging insights derived from users’ LTV and games’ DAU/MAU, this approach can evaluate the model’s effectiveness in identifying more valuable users and games in the early stages. This is a crucial component in marketing strategy.

For the Public Gaming Datasets, we utilize overall player activity levels, rating data, and sales revenue data from the game history as downstream prediction targets. We conducted a comparison between our method and baseline methods, using the public history gaming datasets from the previous 14 days to predict the future intervals of 1, 7, and 15 days.

For the hyperparameters, we opted for an ADAM optimizer with a learning rate of 0.001. Regarding the learning rate, we experimented with values of 0.01, 0.05, 0.001, and 0.0005. A learning rate of 0.001 exhibited a continuous decay in validation loss and yielded the best metrics. Additionally, we employed a batch size of 320 and conducted training for 100 epochs. We implemented callbacks such as ReduceLROnPlateau and EarlyStopping to generalize the model and efficiently converge to the global minima.

With the collected public and industrial datasets, we generated 10-fold time-series cross-validation sets to ensure the credibility of the results. The data in each fold was split chronologically into train, validation, and test sets with a ratio of $6 : 2 : 2 ,$ to prevent target leakage in the time-sensitive environment. In addition, we separated the new-user data from the test dataset as an additional experimental condition.

# 5.2 Performance Comparison

To address RQ1, we perform experiments to compare the proposed model with the baseline methods using both on Industrial Gaming Datasets and Public Gaming Datasets. As shown in Table 2 and Table 3, our proposed GameTrail outperforms the baseline in both metrics for the two application scenarios. Firstly, the classical statistical method RFM can still achieve usable predictive performance for games and users with existing interaction history. However, since this method cannot leverage the individual attributes data, it cannot be applied to the behavioral prediction requirements of new games and new users. By considering additional attribute information from games and users, feature-based prediction algorithms such as Twostage XGBoost and WhalesDetector can achieve better predictive performance compared to statistical methods. The sequence models TSUR and MSDP consider historical interaction sequence data from two perspectives, respectively. TSUR proposes a multi-channel wavelet transform to extract richer temporal data features of user revenue, while MSDP uses a probabilistic model based on temporal variational inference to uncover hidden user interests.

In comparison, our GameTrail model attains superior performance across all metrics in various datasets and tasks. The leading aspect of GameTrail is its consideration of the continuous potential impact brought about by the games’ long-term operational strategies in the gaming industry. Simultaneously, it incorporates a significant amount of game-side attribute data and market operation data. In addition, we further formalized the evolution of game stages and changes in user interests, which underscores the importance of online dynamic learning updates for algorithms and models.

# 5.3 Ablation Study

To answer RQ2, we evaluate our algorithm under different lengths of data input and different model structures for a more in-depth algorithm performance validation, as shown in Table 4. For model structures, we compare three variants of our approach, including (a) cross indicates using only the Latent Encoder modular for downstream task prediction; (b) dynamic indicates using only the Dynamic Encoder modular while considering a dynamic update module for downstream task prediction and averaging the results; (c) both denote our complete model. Finally, we test the performance of our model with input lengths of 10 days and 30 days, respectively.

Observing the performance among different variants, we can summarize the order based on the average results as cross < dynamic < both, as shown in Table 4. Firstly, the average prediction results for an input length of 30 days are slightly better than those for an input length of 10 days. This implies that as the interaction between games and users grows, we can obtain more accurate embedding representations. Additionally, we observed that when the prediction window is set to 1 day, the performance of "cross" is slightly better than "dynamic." However, as the prediction window becomes longer (7 days, 30 days), "dynamic" performs better. This suggests that the stage transitions of games and users exhibit a certain degree of smooth dynamic behavior. The practical use of feature-based model algorithms with daily updates can also capture this smooth dynamic behavior to some extent.
