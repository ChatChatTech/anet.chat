LAN ZHANG∗, University of Science and Technology of China and Institute of Artificial Intelligence, Hefei Comprehensive National Science Center, China

DAREN ZHENG, MU YUAN, FENG HAN, ZHENGTAO WU, MENGJING LIU, and XIANG-YANG LI, University of Science and Technology of China, China

To tap-in the gold-mine of data generated by IoT devices with unprecedented volume and value, there is an urgent need to efficiently and accurately label raw sensor data. To this end, we explore and leverage the hidden connections among the multimodal data collected by various sensing devices, and propose to let different modal data complement and learn from each other. But it is challenging to align and fuse multimodal data without knowing their perception (and thus the correct labels). In this work, we propose MultiSense, a paradigm for automatically mining potential perception, cross labelling each modal data, and then updating the learning models for recognizing human activity to achieve higher accuracy or even recognize new activities. We design innovative solutions for segmenting, aligning, and fusing multimodal data from different sensors, as well as model updating mechanism. We implement our framework and conduct comprehensive evaluations on a rich set of data. Our results demonstrate that MultiSense significantly improves the data usability and the power of the learning models. With 9 diverse activities performed by users, our framework automatically labels multimodal sensing data generated by five different sensing mechanisms (video, smart watch, smartphone, audio, and wireless-channel) with an average accuracy 98.5%. Furthermore, it enables models of some modalities to learn unknown activities from other modalities and greatly improves the activity recognition ability.

A great number of machine learning models are designed to understand the data of different modalities, including videos [30, 32], audio data [2, 45], motion sensor data [4, 18, 23], and wireless signals [22, 26]. However, in practice there are several significant limitations restricting these intelligent sensing applications. Firstly, unimodal data only captures partial information of the whole scenario and often results in low understanding accuracy or even failure. For example, a video can only capture visual information of activities, while an audio can capture extra information implied by speech and sound. Moreover, different data streams may experience temporal or spatial capability diversities, e.g., video streams from different cameras often have different spatial coverage, and wireless signal or audio signal may not capture some activities due to long distances to subjects. In the multimedia field, many recent efforts have been devoted to utilizing multimodal data to achieve more reliable results for video analysis [8, 36, 39], emotion recognition [5, 20], human activity recognition [19, 40, 41], etc. Most of those models work in a supervised manner and focus on multimodal data from the same objects. There are still large spaces for exploring how to make better use of multimodal data, especially massive unlabelled multimodal data from multiple objects. Secondly, existing unimodal or multi-modal machine learning models are often designed for some specific task, and trained to classify data into limited predefined categories. Self-evolving machine learning systems are highly desired by applications in complex environments. Thirdly, to fully enjoy the power of existing sophisticated models, there is an urgent demand for large amounts of multimodal labelled data. Considering highly diverse activities and events in real life, however, manual annotation is time-consuming and labor-intensive. For some modality data (e.g., motion sensor data and wireless signal), it’s quite difficult for both people and models to label. Though many existing works utilize labelled multimodal data samples, few works address how to segment and label multimodal data streams.

To address these problems, taking advantages of the intrinsic correlation among multimodal data, we came up with the idea of leveraging recognition results of some modalities to automatically label unrecognized data of other modalities (referred to as cross-labelling) and thenhelping existing models of some modalities evolve to learn unknown categories as well as to achieve better recognition accuracy (referred to as cross-learning). In this work, we focus on a typical sensing scenario where multimodal data are collected continuously for multiple objects by various sensing devices, e.g., smart home and smart healthcare. To achieve the vision of cross-labelling and cross-learning, we need to address following critical challenges:

(1) How to align multimodal data streams from the same person? An intuitive idea is to use human and device identification information, e.g., device ID for wearable devices, and face recognition by CV-based methods. However, such identity information is usually sensitive and hard to obtain, especially in open environment. It is even more difficult to pre-establish correspondence between people identities and device IDs. Thus we need to design a general alignment method with only unlabelled multimodal multi-person data.

(2) How to correctly segment continuous streams into actions automatically? Segmenting data streams by activity is a critical operation for data labelling and learning [14]. However, there is a “segment and prediction” paradox: for better segmentation of data stream, we need to recognize the activity described by each segment; for better activity recognition, we need partition the data such that each segment only contains the data for one activity, no more no less.

(3) How to solve strongly conflicting outputs of machine learning models of different modalities and determine proper labels for unrecognized segments? Pre-trained models of different modalities may provide strongly conflicting predictions for the same activity. The conflicts could come from two sources: the incompetent initial capabilities of machine learning models, and the different spatial-coverage and sensing capabilities of different sensing devices. We need carefully address the conflicts to improve prediction and labelling.

(4) How to update existing learning models? After obtaining newly labelled data samples, we need update existing models to learn new categories or increase the accuracy of existing models for recognizing certain categories. Since not all data modalities and models are suitable for recognizing all kinds of activities, we need a data quality evaluation method to decide which data is used to update the model to extend its scope and improve its accuracy.

# 2 PROBLEM FORMULATION & DESIGN OVERVIEW

# 2.1 Concept and Problem Definition

We explore a typical sensing scenario, e.g., Fig. 1, where various sensing devices collect multimodal data for multiple persons. Co-located sensors may capture different features of the same activity and produce highly correlated multimodal data. Inspired by this insight, we propose a new sensing data labelling and learning paradigm, MultiSense, which leverages the sensing power of multimodal data and recognition power of multiple learning models to enable automatic data labelling (i.e., cross-labelling) and improve model capability $( \mathrm { i . e . , }$ crosslearning). Moreover, better recognition accuracy for each activity can be achieved by fusing results of multiple models.

Formally, let $\mathbb { A } = \{ a _ { 1 } , \cdots , a _ { N } \}$ be the universe of activities, which contains ?? activities under study. There are ?? modalities of sensing data. In this work, we consider four modal sensing data: 1) video captured by surveillance cameras; 2) motion sensor data collected by smart watches or smartphones; 3) audio data captured by microphones of smart watches or surveillance cameras; 4) wireless signals captured by APs. During initialization, for each modality there exist a small set of labelled data $\mathbb { T } _ { i }$ for a subset of activities $\mathbb { A } _ { i } \subseteq \mathbb { A }$ . Here ?? is the index for the ??-th data modality. Using data set $\mathbb { T } _ { i }$ as the training data, a model (or a set of models) $M _ { i }$ can be trained to recognize activities in $\mathbb { A } _ { i }$ with a certain degree of accuracy, which indicates the initial capability of the learning model. When the system is working, data streams of different modalities arrive continuously, which contain information of various activities performed by multiple persons. Let $\mathbb { D } _ { i } ^ { j }$ be the ?? -th data stream of the ??-th modality.

The objective of MultiSense is threefold: 1) partition each data stream $\mathbb { D } _ { i } ^ { j }$ to segments $\mathbb { D } _ { i } ^ { j } [ t _ { s } , t _ { e } ]$ so that each segment contains only information for one action, e.g., one walking step or one keystroke. Here $t _ { s }$ and $t _ { e }$ are the start and end time of this data segment; 2) labelling those data segments with high accuracy; 3) updating existing models using newly labelled data to enhance ability of these machine learning models. The first two objectives are to produce high-quality multimodal labelled data in an automatic and continuous way. The third objective is to achieve self-evolution for the multimodal activity recognition system. The main challenges and opportunity both arise from the fact that the initial ability of models of different modalities could vary widely.

# 2.2 System Architecture

As the initialization, each sensing device needs to register the MultiSense service and uploads sensing data in order to get recognizing results in return. Each of the machine learning models, which can be deployed at the sensing device or the server, also needs to register the service and provides its output results in order to learn unknown activities and increase prediction accuracy. Note that unknown activities here do not mean activities that are completely new to all modalities, but unknown to some modalities. The overview of MultiSense is presented in Fig. 1, which includes the following main components.

(1) Preprocessing. To facilitate multimodal data segmentation and cross-modality labelling, we first split the complex data streams which contain activity information of multiple persons to multiple data streams. As a result, each data stream only contains information of one person. Then we transform all data streams to a unified form– time sequences and use a band-pass filter to eliminate noises. Specially, for a video stream, we propose a skeleton recognition based method to split data of multiple persons and transform a video stream to time sequences of wrist/hip movement. An audio data splitting method is also designed to separate sounds of different activities on the same track. Motionless segments are also detected with a simple threshold based method.

(2) Multimodal segment proposing. We need partition each time sequences data into segments so that each segment reflect a complete action, e.g. one step and one keystroke. Facing the raw data without any pre-knowledge of user’s activity, we design a multimodal segment proposing method to output a set of segment hypotheses with high confidence. Extracting feature vectors of all segment hypotheses, we predict their labels (with confidence values) using pre-trained machine learning models.   
(3) Label evaluation. Given a machine learning model, a high-confidence prediction doesn’t necessarily mean a high accuracy. For proposed segments and their predicted labels, we evaluate the reliability of those labels by analyzing their distribution in the feature space.   
(4) Data-person alignment. In a multi-person scenario, before cross-modality labelling, we need to determine which group of data sequences describe activities by the same person. With only incomplete data segments and unreliable predictions, it is difficult to align a bunch of data sequences to their corresponding person correctly. Especially when the models of different modalities have disjoint ability (output disjoint labels), their predictions do not provide any help to grouping multimodal data. To deal with this challenge, we use not only motion evidence but also motionless evidence to achieve high-accuracy cross-modality data-person alignment.   
(5) Prediction aggregation and cross-labelling. Obtaining multimodal data segments of the same person, we design a prediction aggregation mechanism which combines evidences (predictions) of different modalities to make the final decision of the person’s activities. The reliability of each prediction (computed in Step 3) is also taken into consideration. Compared with existing evidence combination rule, our mechanism can deal with strongly conflicting evidences when models’ ability vary greatly. We select segments successfully proposed in the Step 2 and label them.   
(6) Model updating. With newly labelled data segments, we can use them as new training samples to iteratively update existing model to learn unknown activities or increase the recognition accuracy of known activities.

As shown in Fig. 1, newly labelled data can be further used to improve the performance of segment proposing and updated models can be adopted to provide more and better predictions in the next iteration. In this way, MultiSense is a closed-loop sensing system which continues evolving into a more intelligent one. Consequently, our system provides system extensibility where a user can input labelled data (of known categories or unknown categories) from other sources in any iteration. With our design, newly collected labelled data of any modality will empower not only model for this modality but also learning models for other modalities to recognize more activities of the input data.

# 3 PREPROCESSING

We need preprocess all data streams to mitigate the following difficulties. 1) Data streams of different modalities have completely different manifestations. For example, videos are sequences of RGB image frames while other data streams, including motion sensor data, audio data and wireless signal data, are sequences of data points. Since they capture different aspects of activities, it is infeasible to compute their correlations directly. 2) When there are multiple persons in the sensing field performing activities freely, sensing data can be quite noisy and some data stream even contains information of activities of multiple persons. Facing these two difficulties, for preprocessing we propose to transform all data streams to a unified form – time sequences of data points, which enables effective segmentation and cross-modality alignment. Time sequence of data point is a sequence of data point indexed in time order. It can also be referred to time series data or time stamped data. Note that, there still lacks a way to split complex audio and wireless signals of activities of multiple persons. In our cross-sensing system, therefore, audio and wireless signals can only be adopted when there is only one person.

# 3.1 Video to Time Sequences of Points

We propose to transform video to time sequences of data points to facilitate the exploration of connection between a video stream and sequences of other modalities. It requires the sequences to capture key movement information of activities. We notice that wearable devices usually are attached or close to body joints. Inspired by this insight, we extract 2D skeletons of people in a video [3, 27, 35] and track the movement of joints to generate a time sequence for each person. The skeleton extraction model also reports the people number ?? . If there are multiple persons in the video, we split a video to multiple time sequences of positions of wrist and hip.

# 3.2 Data Noise Filtering

The raw time sequences could be very noisy, as shown in the first row in Fig. 3. Considering the frequency limitation of human action, we first use band-pass filter with range [0.2????, 10???? ] to remove the DC components and high-frequency noises, as shown in the second row in Fig. 3. After the band-pass filtering, we can easily detect the motionless parts of each data stream with an amplitude threshold, when a person is standing or sitting still. The motionless parts will not be considered for segmentation and labelling, but they can assist in distinguishing data streams of multiple persons (in Section 4.3).

# 3.3 Model Pre-training

For the initialization, for each modality we have already collected a set of labelled data segments for a subset of activities. Each segment contains one action of a certain activity performed by an individual. For each modality, we extract features of segments using corresponding methods, including Openpose [3, 27, 35], MFCC, Lasagna [18], and Motion-Fi [22]. With these features and labels of data segments, for each modality we can train a classifier to recognize the labelled activities. Note that in this work, we train two models for motion sensor data. One is for data collected by smartphones, the other is for data collected by smart watches.

# 4 CROSS LABELLING

As the data arrives continuously, by preprocessing, we transform each data stream $\mathbb { D } _ { i } ^ { k }$ into a time sequence $\mathbb { S } _ { i } ^ { k }$ Here $\mathbb { D } _ { i } ^ { k }$ is the ??-th data stream of the ??-th modality. In an uncontrolled environment, people can perform activities freely. As a result, each time sequence may contain multiple diverse activities. Our goal is to process each time sequence $\mathbb { S } _ { i } ^ { k }$ to obtain a set of labelled segments $S _ { i } ^ { k } = \{ \hat { S } _ { i } ^ { k } [ 1 ] , S _ { i } ^ { k } [ 2 ] , \cdots \}$ , where $S _ { i } ^ { \lessgtr } [ n ] = ( \bar { \mathbb { S } } _ { i } ^ { k } [ t _ { s } ^ { n } : t _ { e } ^ { n } ] , L _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ] )$ . Here $t _ { s } ^ { n }$ and $t _ { e } ^ { n }$ are the start time and end time for the ??-th segment $S _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ]$ . Each segment should only contain a single action, thus segments from the same sequence should not overlap temporally . $L _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ] \in \mathbb { A }$ is the label of this segment, which is determined by MultiSense and should be as accurate as possible. This task is nontrivial due to the ’segment and prediction’ paradox: we need to segment data sequence by activity before we feed the data into a model to predict activities that the data contains. Moreover, the initial ability of pre-trained models are limited and vary from one another. They may provide highly conflicting predictions for data of the same activity.

In this work, we propose a strategy to solve the segmentation, alignment, prediction fusing and cross-labelling issues by making full use of existing multimodal labelled data and pre-trained models, as well as the correlations among multimodal data. First, we design a multimodal segment proposing algorithm to propose a set of segment hypotheses. Second, extracting feature vectors of all proposed segments and predicting their labels using pretrained models, we evaluate the quality/reliability of each prediction. Third, we conduct data-person alignment to group multimodal data describing activities of the same person according to their motion and motionless correlations. Finally, for each person we design a mechanism to aggregate conflicting predictions from multimodal models to achieve high-confidence final decision of the label of each data segment.

# 4.1 Multimodal Segment Proposal

Given a time sequence, an activity can be at any position and last for any duration. To cope with both diversity of different activities and diversity of the same activity performed by different persons, the basic idea of segment proposing is using the historical labelled data segments as templates to conduct search with varying durations on the newly arrived time sequences. Those segments matching one of the templates will be proposed as candidate segments. For those segments not being successfully proposed by existing templates, most of which are segments of unknown activities, will be re-selected and labelled according to their correlated segments in other modalities (in Section 4.4). Specially, we don’t segment audio data using this template proposing way. After filtering, the audio data consists of a number of very short waves (about 100 ms) and long intervals among them. Thus we can easily divide an audio time sequence into segments by detecting those intervals.

# 4.2 Label Evaluation

# 4.3 Data-Person Alignment

When there are $N > 1$ persons, it is necessary to conduct data-person alignment to group multimodal data sequences of the same person together to ensure correct cross-modality labelling. The importance of data-person alignment lies in that we need to know which data streams are describing the same activity for cross labelling and prediction aggregation. For example, as shown in Fig. 4, there are two persons, person A stepping and person B squatting. Our system receives two motion sensor data streams from their smartwatches and a video stream containing them. To correctly cross label the motion sensor data and video data, we need to align these data streams to their corresponding person; otherwise, we may mislabel the motion sensor data of person A by the video recognition result of person B. (1) Motion correlation: Generally, multimodal data sequences of the same person should have more consistent activity labels (in both label category and time) than that of different persons. As a result, we can compute the cross-correlations of labels among time sequences to find the optimal ?? matching groups to maximize the average intra-group cross-correlation. In some cases, however, the initial ability of models could be very limited, which results in highly unreliable predicted labels. Especially, when the ability of models are totally different, they output disjoint labels for data sequences of different modalities, which provide no clue to assign sequences to different groups. To tackle this problem, we leverage a very special activity “Motionless”, which can be easily recognized for all data modalities as a cross-modality evidence.   
(2) Motionless correlation: Motionless segments (e.g., standing or sitting still) of different data sequences can be simply detected using amplitude thresholds. Then, multimodal data sequences of the same person should have temporally consistent motionless segments. We find that the motionless correlation is much more reliable than the motion correlation. So we group data sequences according to motionless correlation first and adopt motion correlation only when multiple persons keep moving without any noticeable pause or they pause and restart moving simultaneously. In a corner case where multiple persons perform exactly the same activity synchronously, activity labels of different persons’ data sequences are identical. Then we cannot distinguish data of different persons, however, a wrong grouping won’t cause incorrect crossmodality labelling results. In another rare corner case where multiple persons perform different activities synchronously and unfortunately the output labels for multimodal data sequences are disjoint, we discard those data sequences to guarantee the accuracy of cross-labelling.

# 4.4 Prediction Aggregation and Cross-labelling

Now we obtain groups of multimodal data segments. Each group contains data of an individual. Each segment $S _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ]$ has a prediction result $M _ { i } ( S _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ] ) = \{ ( a _ { j } , c ( a _ { j } ) ) | a _ { j } \in \mathbb { A } _ { i } \}$ with its corresponding discount values $\{ D i s c ( S _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ] , a _ { j } ) \}$ }. For concurrent segments of different modalities, their predictions may be conflicting. For example, at the same moment, the label of the smart watch data could be “running” while the label of the wireless signal is “fighting”. Thus, to achieve accurate activity recognition and cross-labelling, we need to solve the conflicts and make a final decision for each data segment.

A general solution is based on the algorithm follow-the-leader, however, which has been proven that the performance is worse than the best single model [25]. So we turn to aggregate multiple predictions to achieve higher accuracy. Aggregation of multiple models has been explored in different domains, e.g., health care [29], speech [17], image classification [33], and forecasting [28]. These methods are usually data-driven and learningbased, which rely on aligned and labelled training data. Differently, the main purpose of MultiSense is to label the raw data, which means we do not yet have sufficient data to train the aggregation model. Therefore, MultiSense needs no prior knowledge and works as a preceding step for those learning-based aggregation methods.

Here, we cannot directly adopt the traditional D-S theory (Dempster-Shafer evidence theory) [24] to fuse predictions. The combination rule of traditional D-S theory is ineffective to deal with strongly conflicting evidences when multiple models have quite different abilities. For example, a model may not recognize the activity “fighting”, thus won’t output a “fighting” label, which means it gives zero confidence for this category. Since the Dempster rule of combination is based on conjunctive operation (AND), “fighting” will never be selected as the final decision no matter what are the outputs of other models. To solve this combining problem facing highly conflicting evidences, we design a mechanism working as follows:

(1) Given the prediction result $\{ ( a _ { j } , c ( a _ { j } ) ) | a _ { j } \ \in \ \mathbb { \mathbb { A } } _ { i } \}$ of $S _ { i } ^ { k } [ n ]$ by model $M _ { i } ,$ , for each label $a _ { j }$ , instead of defining $m _ { i } ( a _ { j } ) = c ( a _ { j } )$ we define the mass function for model $M _ { i }$ as $m _ { i } ( \{ a _ { j } \} \cup ( \mathbb { A } \setminus \mathbb { A } _ { i } ) ) = c ( a _ { j } )$ . Then $m _ { i } ( \mathbb { A } _ { i } \setminus \{ a _ { j } \} ) = 1 - c ( a _ { j } )$ . Here $\mathbb { A } \setminus \mathbb { A } _ { i }$ represents the ignorance of the model $M _ { i }$ . This mass function reflects that, when a model ???? produces a label $a _ { j }$ for a data segment, with a high probability the true activity is either $a _ { j }$ or an unknown activity for $M _ { i } .$ .

(2) Since the reliability of predictions varies with the ability of models, we reduce the influence of an unreliable prediction by discounting its confidence as

$$
S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ]. c (a _ {j}) = S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ]. c (a _ {j}) \exp (D i s c (S _ {i} ^ {k} [ n ], a _ {j})). \tag {3}
$$

(3) With adjusted confidences we can calculate mass functions for all predictions, each of which is an evidence. For each set of concurrent multimodal segments, we combine their evidences to compute the belief for each decision $a _ { j } \in \mathbb { A } .$ The belief is the probability that $a _ { j }$ is supported by evidences, which is computed as

$$
B e l \left(a _ {j}\right) = \frac {1}{K} \sum_ {\cap \mathbb {A} _ {i} = \left\{a _ {j} \right\}} \prod_ {1 \leq i \leq n} m _ {i} \left(a _ {j}\right), \tag {4}
$$

$$
\text { where } K = 1 - \sum_ {\bigcap \mathbb {A} _ {i} = \emptyset} \prod_ {1 \leq i \leq n} m _ {i} (a _ {j}).
$$

Here ?? denotes the number of model ??. We choose the decision with highest belief as the final prediction of all data segments in this concurrent set.

# 5 CROSS LEARNING

As MultiSense runs, newly labelled multimodal data segments are produced by cross-labelling continuously. We take those fresh data segments as training samples to iteratively update existing models, making them learn unknown activities or increase the accuracy of known activities. There are three principles for updating models. First, the updated models’ prediction accuracy for known activities should not deteriorate greatly; Second, the prediction accuracy for new activity categories should be as high as possible; Last, frequent updating should be avoided for system stability and efficiency. Obeying these principles, we need select a set of labelled data before we use it for model updating. Specifically, for each modality, the process of each update iteration includes the following four steps.

Step 1: data collection. We wait to collect a certain amount of training samples for at least one activity category. The minimum amount of samples required for a good model training is an empirical value.

# 6.3 Cross labelling

For cross-labelling, the goal is to label the data as accurately as possible. In this section, we evaluate the crosslabelling accuracy in the single-person scenario, which is mainly determined by the accuracy of prediction aggregation.

6.3.1 Multimodal data segmentation. We compare our multimodal data segmentation method with the following two popular methods:

(1) Sliding window: Each time sequence is segmented by a sliding window of 2s.   
(2) Envelope: we first compute the envelope of a time sequence, then use the mean values of the upper and lower envelopes as thresholds to discretize the series, where the sensing values are larger than the

corresponding mean values are set to 1s and others are set to 0s. The time sequence is sliced into segments from where the discrete value equals to 0.

We use three segmentation methods to our collected multimodal data and measure the average accuracy of classifiers trained with data segmented by different methods. Since the feature extractors and classifiers are the same, the more precise the segmentation is, the higher the classification accuracy should be. Fig. 5 shows the results with 5-fold cross validation in Case 1 to 4. Note that audio data can be easily segmented by detecting intervals, so we omit it in this experiment. # 6.5 Data-Person Alignment.

When there are multiple persons (e.g., two persons in our experiment) in the room, we need to first group multimodal data streams by person. In this experiment, we align data streams of smart watch, smartphone and video using both motionless and motion correlations. We aim to match multimodal data stream maintaining a high accuracy rate and utilize as much data as possible. Thus we choose data utilization rate and matching accuracy as assessment metrics. Fig.13(a) shows the influence of data stream length on the alignment accuracy. We can see that in all cases the alignment accuracy increase with longer data sequences, since longer sequences provider richer information for alignment. In Case 2, when the data sequence length is 1, 2, and 3 minutes, the alignment accuracy are 87.2%, 91.9%, and 95.3% respectively. Fig.13(b) presents the matching accuracy and data utilization ratio when aligning data sequences of two modalities. As we mentioned in Section 4.3, we discard segments which we cannot match with high confidence. Thus the data utilization ratio indicates what percentage of data are reserved. In Case 3, there are 3 overlapping activities between smart watch and smartphone models, 3 overlapping activities between video and smartphone models, and only 1 overlapping activity between smart watch and video models, so the accuracy and data utilization ratio for smart watch and video data sequences are lower than the other two pairs of models. Fig.13(c) illustrates data sequences alignment performance using only cross-modal motion correlations and all three initial models in Case 1 to 3. The accuracy and data utilization rate are both nearly 100% in Case 1, and then decreases progressively from Case 1 to 3. Specially, Case 4 is the corner case that there is no overlapping category among three models, so there won’t be any common label can be utilized for measure cross-modal correlations. Fig.13(d) gives alignment performance using both motion and motionless correlations. In all four cases, the alignment accuracy are higher than 90%, which is significantly better than the results using only motion correlations. In Case 4, the accuracy achieves 100% and the data utilization rate is 57.8%. The reason for the high accuracy is that we use only motionless correlations for alignment, which is highly reliable, and discard segments cannot be aligned by motionless correlations, since there is no motion correlation. With accurate data-person alignment results, the performance of cross-labelling and model updating in the two-person scenario is quite similar to that in the single-person scenario.

# 7 RELATED WORK

Multimodal applications and models. Massive multimodal data are being generated by various devices in a broad range of application scenarios[43]. The benefit from multimodal data is that we can get diversity view and complementary information compared with unimodal data. Also due to sensing system heterogeneity, the performance of unimodal model may have sharply lower classification accuracy in unconstrained and real-world conditions while multimodal modal can alleviate this situation [21, 44]. In addition when one or several modality data is missing, multimodal models can still operate [13]. A series of efforts have been devoted to utilizing multimodal data to achieve better understanding of scenarios and enhance the robustness or security of models, e.g.. video analysis [8, 36], emotion recognition [5, 20], human activity recognition [19, 40, 41], mobile positioning [6, 46], side channel attack [9] and rumor detection [11]. Those works mainly focus on how to fuse features of heterogeneous multimodal data to achieve better prediction accuracy. Typically, for spatial temporal sensing data, Supreme [16] proposed a spatial-temporal reconstruction network to solve how to represent complex spatial-temporal correlations and external factors with spatial-temporal residual block and external factor fusion module in fine-grained radio map reconstruction problem. DeepFusion [40] presented a unified multi-sensor deep learning framework, which can combine different sensors’ information weighted by the quality of their data and incorporate cross-sensor correlations to improve the sensing performance. GlobalFusion [19] proposed a global attention mechanism for applying deep learning techniques to IoT application. Global attention can correlate global context derived from higher level features of neural network with local sensor features to find informative ones and filter out the unrelated noise, which is different from previous attention mechanisms. There are also many work exploring aggregating multiple models in different domains, e.g., health care [29], speech [17], image classification [33], and forecasting [28]. Most of those multimodal and multiple model aggregation methods are data-driven and learning-based, which require well aligned and labelled training samples. Differently, we consider one of the major challenges for the wide adoption of those methods, that is how to efficiently and accurately align and label multimodal data. Therefore, MultiSense needs no prior knowledge to learn the fusion/aggregation model and can work as a preceding step for those learning-based aggregation methods.

Multimodal data annotation. Manually labelling data is time-consuming and difficult[15], especially for multimodal data streams from multiple objects. One way to tackle this problem is to design models to make full use of limited labelled data or noisy labelled data. Huang et al. [10] propose an annotation framework for learning multimodal joint embedding space. The real-world datasets may be noisy, resulting in not ideal classification accuracy. SGSF (Small groups based serial fusion) [34] is proposed for removing low-density noise samples whilst keeping the high-density outliers samples without destroying the data distribution. Semi-supervised learning (SSL) is a widely adopted methodology for generating pseudo-labels. Most existing SSL models only consider a single modality [37, 42]. Recently, a few works are tailored for some specific multimodal scenarios. For example, a deep generative model by semi-supervised learning is proposed for incomplete multimodal emotional data [5]. Another way is to minimize data annotation cost by using active learning, however, most of which focus on unimodal scenarios, e.g., [1, 4]. There are also some works addressing knowledge transferring across modalities. RecycleML [38] proposes a cross-modal training approach, which requires manually specifying the objective of transferring. All those works assume that multimodal data streams of the same object have already been correctly aligned and segmented, which however are nontrivial in complex environments.

In this work, we address the multimodal data auto-labelling and model auto-updating issues in more general and complex scenarios (e.g., smart home and smart healthcare) where multimodal data are collected continuously for multiple objects by various sensing devices, and the initial ability of models of different modalities could vary widely. Our proposed segmentation, alignment and labelling techniques can work as the pre-operations or complementary of existing multimodal models, and our paradigm can also utilize existing models in a black-box manner.

# 8 CONCLUSION

In this work, we presented MultiSense for automatically labelling multi-modal data collected by a variety of sensors monitoring certain objects. It turns a large set of unlabelled data into gold-mine for machine learning models and improves the prediction accuracy of these learning models using cross-learning and fusion. Our comprehensive experiments demonstrated the effectiveness and efficiency of our proposed framework. There are still several challenging tasks left for fully maximizing the advantages of this framework. The first challenge is to improve the robustness and efficiency for aligning the data from multiple objects in more complex scenarios. The second is to further improve the labelling and learning accuracy, especially for the audio data and the backscattered wireless signal data. The third is to reduce the cost of data preprocessing.
