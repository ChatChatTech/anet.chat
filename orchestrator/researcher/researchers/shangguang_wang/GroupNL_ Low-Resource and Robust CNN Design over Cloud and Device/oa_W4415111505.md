# GroupNL: Low-Resource and Robust CNN Design over Cloud and Device

Chuntao Ding, Jianhang Xie, Junna Zhang, Salman Raza, Shangguang Wang, Jiannong Cao

Abstract—Deploying Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models on ubiquitous Internet of Things (IoT) devices in a cloud-assisted manner to provide users with a variety of high-quality services has become mainstream. Most existing studies speed up model cloud training/on-device inference by reducing the number of convolution (Conv) parameters and floating-point operations (FLOPs). However, they usually employ two or more lightweight operations (e.g., depthwise Conv, 1 × 1 cheap Conv) to replace a Conv, which can still affect the model’s speedup even with fewer parameters and FLOPs. To this end, we propose the Grouped NonLinear transformation generation method (GroupNL), leveraging data-agnostic, hyperparameters-fixed, and lightweight Nonlinear Transformation Functions (NLFs) to generate diversified feature maps on demand via grouping, thereby reducing resource consumption while improving the robustness of CNNs. First, in a GroupNL Conv layer, a small set of feature maps, i.e., seed feature maps, are generated based on the seed Conv operation. Then, we split seed feature maps into several groups, each with a set of different NLFs, to generate the required number of diversified feature maps with tensor manipulation operators and nonlinear processing in a lightweight manner without additional Conv operations. We further introduce a sparse GroupNL Conv to speed up by reasonably designing the seed Conv groups between the number of input channels and seed feature maps. Experiments conducted on benchmarks and on-device resource measurements demonstrate that the GroupNL Conv is an impressive alternative to Conv layers in baseline models. Specifically, on Icons-50 dataset, the accuracy of GroupNL-ResNet-18 is 2.86% higher than ResNet-18; on ImageNet-C dataset, the accuracy of GroupNL-EfficientNet-ES achieves about 1.1% higher than EfficientNet-ES. In addition, we verified the efficiency of GroupNL-based models in terms of cloud training and on-device inference.

Index Terms—Internet of Things, cloud computing, cloud-assisted, CNNs.

These methods can reduce the number of model parameters and FLOPs, but the lightweight operations (depthwise Conv and cheap Conv) introduced affect the model’s cloud training/on-device inference speed. To this end, this paper aims to propose a CNN design method that improves model robustness by increasing feature map diversity, while reducing model complexity and avoiding the introduction of lightweight operations such as depthwise Conv and cheap Conv to achieve better training/inference speed.

First, this paper proposes the Grouped NonLinear transformation method, GroupNL. In the GroupNL CNN, partially trainable filters are configured within each Conv layer, which are referred to as seed filters or seed Conv. The feature maps produced via seed filters are called seed feature maps. Subsequently, we split the seed feature maps into many small groups. The remaining feature maps can be generated using diverse NLFs, each configured with different hyperparameters for each group of seed feature maps. The hyperparameters of NLFs are fixed, ensuring transmission friendliness when cloud-assisted. The seed feature maps and the generated feature maps are concatenated into the Conv layer’s output. In particular, with distributed data parallel training on ImageNet-1K, the GroupNL ResNet-101 achieves a 53% speedup over vanilla ResNet-101 while maintaining accuracy. We also deploy GroupNL models on resource-constrained devices (Raspberry Pi 4B), and ondevice measurements show that the GroupNL models outperform in terms of inference speed, memory usage, power, and energy consumption.

# 2 RELATED WORK

This section briefly introduces cloud-device training and deployment and the Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) models that inspire our work.

# 2.1 Cloud-Device Training and Deployment

Due to the limited resources of IoT devices, cloud-device collaborative training and deployment of CNN models have received widespread attention. The existing architectures can be classified into three categories:

Cloud-Only. The cloud-only architecture trains and deploys the CNN models on the cloud server. The user sends data and service requests to the cloud server, and after the cloud server processes the data, it sends the results to the users [19]–[22]. For example, Liu et al. [22] proposed to deploy the CNN model on the cloud server, and the device obtains recognition services by uploading the collected data to the cloud server. They also process data on edge servers [23]–[28] to reduce the amount of data uploaded to the cloud. However, the quality of services of cloud-only architecture is fully constrained by the network conditions.

Cloud-Device Collaboration. The second architecture splits the CNN model into two parts: partial CNN layers are deployed on the cloud server, and other parts on the IoT devices. The cloud and IoT devices are collaborative training and inference of the split CNN model [29]–[31]. For example, Kang et al. [29] proposed to divide the CNN into a head running on the device and a tail running on the cloud, and decide the split point based on the load of the device and the conditions of the cloud and model. Stefanos et al. [31] proposed a cloud-device collaborative inference method for the cloud and device with compression technologies to reduce the parameter exchange. However, cloud-device collaborative methods have to search for the optimal partitioning points labor-intensively for different CNNs.

Cloud-Assisted Training and Device Deployment. The third architecture is that the cloud server first trains the CNN model and then sends the trained CNN model to the IoT device for deployment [12]–[14], [32]–[34]. We refer to it as the Cloud-Assisted Architecture. For example, Ding et al. [12] proposed to train a CNN model containing a few learnable parameters on the cloud server and then send the trained CNN model to the IoT device for deployment. Lu et al. [33] proposed to train a lightweight vision transformer model on the cloud server, and then send the trained vision transformer model to the IoT devices for deployment. Furthermore, they reduce the number of model parameters transmitted from the cloud to IoT devices by replacing the attention mechanism with multi-scale pooling technology.

The first and second architectures are closely related to network conditions. When the network connection is unstable or unavailable, IoT devices will provide poor-quality services or even be unable to provide services. The proposed GroupNL is based on the cloud-assisted training and IoT device deployment architecture.

Because of the convolution operation’s excellent featureextraction capabilities, many popular CNN models have been proposed. For example, Simonyan et al. # 3 THE PROPOSED METHOD: GROUPNL

We introduce GroupNL, a low-resource and robust CNN design method for cloud-assisted IoT deployment, where models are trained in the cloud and deployed on resourceconstrained devices. The GroupNL method addresses two key challenges: the demand for real-life resource-efficient training/inference, which prior lightweight alternatives have not fully met, and the need for robustness against realworld corruptions like adverse weather.

We describe the proposed GroupNL in detail as follows: (i) preliminaries in Section 3.1, (ii) detailed design of GroupNL in Section 3.2, and (iii) training and inference acceleration analysis of GroupNL in Section 3.3.

# 3.1 Preliminaries

We illustrate our motivation by analyzing the most relevant works, the nonlinear transformation method MonoCNN [12], and Ghost-like Conv, including Ghost-Net [17] and SineFM [18], respectively.

Given the input feature maps $\pmb { x } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { h \times w \times c _ { \mathrm { i n } } }$ and output feature maps $\pmb { y } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { h \times w ^ { ' } \times c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } }$ of this Conv layer, where the h and w are the spatial dimensions of input feature maps, and $h ^ { ' }$ and w′ for output feature maps. $c _ { \mathrm { i n } } , c _ { \mathrm { o u t } }$ are the number of input and output channels, respectively.

For a detailed description, we introduce two concepts: (i) Seed filters refer to a small number of Conv filters, or referred to as seed Conv; (ii) Seed feature maps refer to feature maps generated by seed filters via seed Conv operation.

We denote seed filters as $W _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { k \times k \times c _ { \mathrm { i n } } \times c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } } .$ , where k is the kernel size. The number of seed filters is $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } ,$ and the number of generated filters is $c _ { \mathrm { g e n . } }$ , where $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } + c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } =$ $c _ { \mathrm { o u t } }$ . We denote an integer reduction ratio r representing the relationship between $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ and $c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } , \mathrm { i . e . , } r = c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } / c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ .

We also set the trainable torch.nn.Module operations in bold in equation, e.g., Conv and BN.

MonoCNN [12]. For a Conv layer, some filters are designated as seed filters, and the remaining filters are generated based on seed filters and one data-agnostic nonlinear transformation function (NLF). Formally, given seed filters and a monomial NLF $\Psi _ { \mathrm { m o n o } } ( \cdot )$ , the MonoConv obtains the remaining filters $W _ { \mathrm { g e n } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { k \times \dot { k } \times c _ { \mathrm { i n } } \times c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } }$ as follows:

$$
\boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { gen }} = \Psi_ {\text { mono }} (\boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { seed }}) , \tag {1}
$$

Then, concatenating the seed filter $W _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ and the generated filters $W _ { \mathrm { g e n } }$ to obtain the filters $W \in \mathbb { R } ^ { k \times k \times c _ { \mathrm { i n } } \times c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } } .$ :

$$
\boldsymbol {W} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { seed }}; \boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { gen }} \right], \tag {2}
$$

Finally, perform a vanilla Conv operation on input feature maps to obtain the output y of MonoConv:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \boldsymbol {W} * \boldsymbol {x} = \operatorname{Concat} [ \boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { seed }}; \Psi_ {\text { mono }} (\boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { seed }}) ] * \boldsymbol {x}, \tag {3}
$$

where ∗ is the Conv operation. The MonoCNN improves the robustness of the CNN model via data-agnostic NLFs for filters, but still with a complete Conv operation.

GhostNet [17]. The Ghost Conv introduces $k \times k$ seed Conv to generate seed feature maps:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }} = \boldsymbol {W} _ {\text { seed }} * \boldsymbol {x} = \underset {k \times k} {\operatorname{Conv}} (\boldsymbol {x}), \tag {4}
$$

where $y _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { h ^ { ' } \times w ^ { ' } \times c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } }$ . For the remaining feature maps, GhostNet introduces additional cheap linear transformation, i.e., the $d \times d$ cheap Conv to process the $\scriptstyle { \pmb { y } } _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ for generating features:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen }} \right] = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}; \underset {d \times d} {\operatorname{Conv}} \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}\right) \right]. \tag {5}
$$

However, the cheap Conv introduced by GhostNet slows down training and inference. Also, the parameters of cheap Conv are trainable, so GhostNet’s robustness is completely restricted to the training data.

SineFM [18]. For a SineFM Conv, similar to the Ghost Conv, given seed filters $W _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ and input feature maps x, we can obtain seed feature maps $\mathbf { \pmb { y } } _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ by the Eq. 4. Then, t-NLFs $\Psi _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } = \{ \Psi _ { i } \| i = 1 , . . . , \bar { t } \}$ process the seed feature maps to obtain expanded feature maps $\pmb { y } _ { \mathrm { e x p } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { h \times w \times t \cdot c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } }$ with more features:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} _ {\exp} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\exp_ {1}}; \dots ; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\exp_ {t}} \right] = \underset {i = 1, \dots , t} {\operatorname{Concat}} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\exp_ {i}} \right], \tag {6}
$$

$$
\pmb {y} _ {\mathrm{exp} _ {i}} = \mathbf {B N} _ {i} \big (\Psi_ {i} (\pmb {y} _ {\mathrm{seed}}) \big),
$$

where $y _ { \mathrm { e x p } _ { i } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { h \times w \times c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } }$ , and $i = 1 , \cdots , t$ . The t is the time of expanding seed feature maps. The expanded feature maps will exceed the number of feature maps required for this layer. Each expanded seed feature maps $\pmb { y } _ { \mathrm { e x p } _ { i } }$ also process a specific $\mathbf { B N } _ { i \cdot } ^ { \bar { } }$ . Then, expanded feature maps are processed by $1 \times 1$ cheap Conv to align the $c _ { \mathrm { g e n } }$ dimensions:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen }} = \underset {1 \times 1} {\mathbf {C o n v}} (\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { exp }}). \tag {7}
$$

Finally, the generated feature maps are concatenated with the seed feature maps to form the output of the SineFM layer as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen }} \right] \\ = \operatorname{Concat} [ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed}}; \underset {1 \times 1} {\mathbf {C o n v}} (\underset {i = 1, \dots , t} {\text {Concat}} [ \mathbf {B N} _ {i} (\Psi_ {i} (\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed}})) ]) ] . \tag {8} \\ \end{array}
$$

# 3.2 Design of GroupNL

# 3.2.1 Grouped Nonlinear Transformation

The aforementioned GhostNet and SineFM methods introduce too much computation in feature maps generation by cheap Conv and BN operations, which result in slow training/inference speed. At the same time, NLFs in SineFM are only t-different types.

Therefore, resource efficiency and robustness need to be further improved. The key challenge is how to increase the diversity of data-agnostic NLFs to improve the robustness of the model without increasing computations.

To this end, we propose the Grouped NonLinear transformation (GroupNL) method. Formally, given the seed filters $W _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ and input feature maps x, we can obtain some seed feature maps $\scriptstyle { \pmb { y } } _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ with the Eq. 4.

Splitting and Grouped Algorithm. We split seed feature maps into g groups in $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ dimension instead of expanding:

$$
\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed}} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed} _ {1}}; \dots ; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed} _ {g}} \right] = \underset {i = 1, \dots , g} {\operatorname{Concat}} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed} _ {i}} \right], \tag {9}
$$

where g is a positive integer, where its modulo result is Mod(cseed, g) = 0, and yseedi ∈ Rh′×w′× cseedg , $\mathrm { { M o d } } ( c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } , g ) = 0 .$ $\begin{array} { r } { \pmb { y } _ { \mathrm { s e e d } _ { i } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \tilde { h } \times w \times \frac { c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } } { g } } , i \ = \ 1 , \cdot \cdot , g . } \end{array}$ For the remaining dimension $c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } = c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } - c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } ,$ , we want to represent it by $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ without expanding the dimension.

We denote a multiplier $\begin{array} { r } { \gamma = \frac { c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } } { c _ { \mathrm { s e a d } } } = r \cdot \frac { c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } } { c _ { \mathrm { i n } } } - 1 } \end{array}$ cseed cgen cin , so each group of seed feature maps should be copied γ times for filling the remaining dimension. Thus, the total number of copied groups of feature maps is $\gamma \cdot g .$ .

Grouped Diverse Nonlinear Transformation. By splitting and copying, we have γ ·g groups of identical seed feature maps, and no additional dimensions are introduced. We can introduce $\gamma \cdot g$ data-agnostic different nonlinear transformations, i.e., diverse NLFs $\Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } = \{ \Psi _ { i , j } \| i = 1 , \ldots , g ; j = 1 , \ldots , \gamma \}$ to these identical groups. Subsequently, for each copied group of seed feature maps, NLFs with different hyperparameter settings are used to generate corresponding feature maps:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen }} = \text { Concat } [ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen } _ {1}}; \dots ; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen } _ {g}} ] = \underset {i = 1, \dots , g} {\text { Concat }} [ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen } _ {i}} ]  , \\ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {gen} _ {i}} = \underset {j = 1, \dots , \gamma} {\operatorname{Concat}} \left[ \Psi_ {i, j} \left(\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text {seed} _ {i}}\right) \right], \tag {10} \\ \end{array}
$$

where i = 1,· · ·,g, j = 1,· · ·,γ, ygeni ∈ Rh′×w′×γ· cseedg , $i = 1 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , g , \ j = 1 , \cdot \cdot \cdot , \gamma , \ y _ { \mathrm { g e n } _ { i } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \not { h } ^ { ' } \times w ^ { ' } \times \gamma \cdot \frac { c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } } { g } }$ and $\begin{array} { r } { \pmb { y } _ { \mathrm { g e n } } \in \mathbb { R } ^ { \dot { h } \times \dot { w } \times \gamma \cdot c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } } , \gamma \cdot c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } = c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } . } \end{array}$

As shown in Fig. 2, we can describe the operations of grouped diverse nonlinear transformation in detail with an example of $c _ { \mathrm { i n } } = 8 , c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } = 1 6 , c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } = 4 , g = 2 .$ . In this case, the number of generated features $c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } = 1 6 - 4 = 1 2 ,$ the multiplier $\gamma = c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } / c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } = 1 2 / 4 = 3 ,$ and introducing $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } | = \stackrel { \scriptscriptstyle } { \gamma } \cdot g = 6$ different NLFs for feature maps generation.

GroupNL Conv. Finally, generated feature maps are concatenated with the seed feature maps to produce the output of the standard GroupNL Conv layer, as follows:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \boldsymbol {y} = \operatorname{Concat} \left[ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}; \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { gen }} \right] \\ = \operatorname{Concat} [ \boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed }}; \underset {i = 1, \dots , g} {\operatorname{Concat}} [ \underset {j = 1, \dots , \gamma} {\operatorname{Concat}} [ \Psi_ {i, j} (\boldsymbol {y} _ {\text { seed } _ {i}}) ] ] ]. \tag {11} \\ \end{array}
$$

    gamma = C_g/C_s # multiplier gamma
    # diverse random hyperparameters of func
    self.hy=nn.Parameter(torch.rand(gamma*g))
    # ensure non-learnable hyperparameters
    self.hy.requires_grad = False

def forward(self, x):
    # feature maps shape [B, C, H, W]
    y_s = self.conv(x) # seed features
    # split seed features into groups
    y_sp = torch.split(y_s, groups_s)
    # copy gamma times feature maps
    y_cp = torch.cat([y_sp[j].repeat([1, gamma, 1, 1]) for j in range(g)])
    # copy hyperparameters of func
    hy = self.hy.repeat_interleave(C_s/g).
    reshape([1, C_g, 1, 1])
    y_g = func(y_cp, hy) # generate C_g features
    return torch.cat([y_s, y_g])

Sparse GroupNL Conv. In addition to the standard GroupNL Conv, we introduce a more lightweight module, termed Sparse GroupNL Conv. In this design, the seed filters $W _ { \mathrm { s e e d } }$ are grouped for thinning, where the number of groups ξ in seed Conv is set to the Greatest Common Divisor (GCD) between $c _ { \mathrm { i n } }$ and $c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } , \ \mathrm { i . e . , } \ \xi = \operatorname { G c d } ( c _ { \mathrm { i n } } , c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } )$ . The number of parameters, FLOPs, and energy consumption in Sparse GroupNL Conv are close to or slightly lower than depthwise Conv, while offering higher computational efficiency, as profiling in Section 4.4.3.

Therefore, Sparse GroupNL Conv can be used to replace depthwise Conv in lightweight models, e.g., MobileNet, EfficientNet, to improve the robustness.

Meanwhile, we observe that Sparse GroupNL Conv preserves performance in large-scale CNNs with bottleneck structures (e.g., ResNet-101), whereas the standard GroupNL Conv is still required for compact models based on basic blocks (e.g., ResNet-18/34).

The PyTorch-like pseudocode of the GroupNL Conv layer is demonstrated in Algorithm 1. As shown, its generating procedures are all tensor manipulation operators, e.g., splitting torch.split, concatenation torch.cat, copying torch.repeat and torch.repeat interleave, and dataagnostic and random nonlinear transformation operations, without any extra torch.nn.Module (Conv and BN) compared with GhostNet in Eq. 5 and SineFM in Eq. 8.

# 3.2.2 Diversities of Nonlinearities and Feature Maps

The number of NLFs in GroupNL is $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } | = \gamma \cdot g ,$ , which is usually larger than the fixed number of t NLFs in SineFM $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } | = \bar { t } ,$ , which act on all seed feature maps to expand or limit feature diversity.

Diversities of Data-agnostic NLFs. Assuming there are 64 seed feature maps, and the number of output channels is $c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } = 2 5 6$ , we can use different NLFs to generate the remaining 192 feature maps.

For SineFM, assuming $t = 5 ,$ it needs to generate $t { \cdot } c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } =$ $5 \cdot 6 4 = 3 2 0$ expanding features and utilizes a cheap Conv to align them into 192 features. So, the number of NLFs is 5.

For GroupNL, the $\gamma = c _ { \mathrm { g e n } } / c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } = 1 9 2 / 6 4 = 3 .$ For $g = 2 ,$ the number of different NLFs is $\left| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } \right| = \gamma \cdot g = 3 \cdot 2 = 6 ;$ for g = 4 is $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } | = \gamma \cdot g = 3 \cdot 4 = 1 2 ;$ for $g = 8$ is $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } | = \gamma \cdot g =$ 3 · 8 = 24; and for the fully grouped g = 64 is $| \Psi _ { \mathrm { g n l } } | = \gamma \cdot g =$ $3 \cdot 6 4 { = } 1 9 2 \gg | \Psi _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } | = 5 .$ .

Compared with the SineFM, the diversity of NLFs is significantly improved without increasing the number of feature maps in GroupNL.

# 3.3 Training & Inference Acceleration Analysis

Cloud Training Acceleration. For cloud-assisted CNN models, the multi-GPU training on cloud servers is mainly based on data parallelism. In practical multi-GPU training, such as Data Parallelism (DP) [37] and Distributed Data Parallelism (DDP) [38], the bandwidth of inter-GPU memory data transfers is an important constraint on the training speed compared to the powerful and fast computation kernels.

So, the number of gradients can be further optimized by reducing inter-GPU scatter and by optimizing different data parallelism levels. Assuming each trainable parameter in a CNN model maintains a gradient, the number of gradients G corresponds to the number of trainable parameters P. For N-GPUs DP, one iteration data communication in the main GPU is $( N - 1 ) \mathcal { G } ;$ for the rest N − 1 GPUs are $( N - 1 ) \mathcal { G } ,$ where each GPU sends G. For N-GPUs DDP, one iteration data communication in a single GPU is $\textstyle { \frac { 2 ( N - { \mathrm { i } } ) } { N } } { \mathcal { G } }$ . Similar to lightweight models, GroupNL essentially achieves training acceleration by reducing the P (and thus the G).

Based on this premise, we analyze the theoretical gradient exchange in multi-GPUs memory of vanilla CNN, GhostNet, SineFM, and GroupNL in training.

Ignoring the bias, for a vanilla Conv layer, the number of parameters and gradients can be described as follows:

$$
\mathcal {P} _ {\text { conv }} = \mathcal {G} _ {\text { conv }} = c _ {\text { in }} c _ {\text { out }} k ^ {2}. \tag {12}
$$

For a Ghost Conv layer, the number of parameters and gradients from the seed Conv and d×d cheap Conv are:

$$
\mathcal {P} _ {\text {ghost}} = \mathcal {G} _ {\text {ghost}} = c _ {\text {in}} c _ {\text {seed}} k ^ {2} + c _ {\text {seed}} c _ {\text {gen}} d ^ {2} \frac {1}{c _ {\text {seed}}} \tag {13}
$$

$$
= c _ {\mathrm{in}} c _ {\mathrm{out}} k ^ {2} \frac {1}{r} + c _ {\mathrm{out}} \frac {r - 1}{r} d ^ {2},
$$

and the number of parameters and gradients for the SineFM from the seed Conv, 1×1 cheap Conv, and BN, are:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{sinefm}} = \mathcal {G} _ {\mathrm{sinefm}} = c _ {\mathrm{in}} c _ {\mathrm{seed}} k ^ {2} + t c _ {\mathrm{seed}} c _ {\mathrm{gen}} \frac {1}{c _ {\mathrm{seed}}} + 2 t c _ {\mathrm{seed}} \\ = c _ {\text {in}} c _ {\text {out}} k ^ {2} \frac {1}{r} + c _ {\text {out}} \frac {t (r + 1)}{r}. \tag {14} \\ \end{array}
$$

The proposed GroupNL Conv layer only contains the learnable standard seed filters and learnable sparse seed filters, assuming $\xi \ = \ \operatorname { G c d } ( c _ { \mathrm { i n } } , c _ { \mathrm { s e e d } } ) .$ , and the number of parameters and gradients are shown in Eq. 15:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{gnl}} = \mathcal {G} _ {\mathrm{gnl}} = c _ {\text {in}} c _ {\text {seed}} k ^ {2} = c _ {\text {in}} c _ {\text {out}} k ^ {2} \frac {1}{r}, \tag {15} \\ \mathcal {P} _ {\mathrm{gnl}} ^ {\mathrm{sp}} = \mathcal {G} _ {\mathrm{gnl}} ^ {\mathrm{sp}} = c _ {\mathrm{in}} c _ {\mathrm{seed}} k ^ {2} \frac {1}{\xi} = c _ {\mathrm{in}} c _ {\mathrm{out}} k ^ {2} \frac {1}{r \xi}. \\ \end{array}
$$

Noticeably, the relation is $\mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { g n l } } ^ { \mathrm { s p } } \leq \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { g n l } } = c _ { \mathrm { i n } } c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } k ^ { 2 } \frac { 1 } { r } <$ min $\{ \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } } , \dot { \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { g h o s t } } } , \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } \} _ { \mathrm { . } }$ , i.e., the number of gradients for optimization in GroupNL is minimal compared to the rest CNN design methods of $\mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } } , \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { g h o s t . } }$ , and $\mathscr { G } _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } }$ . The sparse GroupNL also significantly reduces the gradient exchange by a factor of $( r \check { \xi } ) ^ { - 1 }$ compared to the $\mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } }$ .

Notably, it seems that Ghost Conv and SineFM also have a small number of gradients with $\mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { g h o s t } } < \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } }$ and $\mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } < \mathcal { G } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } } ,$ can also accelerate training in theory. However, the evaluation in Table 8 shows that the GhostNet and SineFM are even slower than the vanilla CNN. For this phenomenon, we consider this to be due to the introduction of more computational nodes involved in the backward updating, such as cheap Conv and BN, which leads to slower training instead. The GroupNL, on the other hand, does not introduce new computational nodes through tensor manipulation operations such as torch.cat, torch.split, torch.repeat and torch.repeat interleave, thus also ensures acceleration of training.

On-Device Inference Efficiency. For a fixed feature map size wh, the computational cost in FLOPs is proportional to the number of parameters, resulting in $\mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { g n l } } ^ { \mathrm { s p } } \doteq \mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { g n l } } =$ $c _ { \mathrm { i n } } c _ { \mathrm { o u t } } k ^ { 2 } \frac { 1 } { r } ~ < ~ \operatorname* { m i n } \{ \mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { c o n v } } , \mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { g h o s t } } , \mathcal { F } _ { \mathrm { s i n e f m } } \}$ , confirming that GroupNL Conv requires significantly fewer computations than other Convs. Furthermore, GroupNL employs the fewest torch.nn.Module, contributing to its theoretically superior inference efficiency on devices. The module-level profiling in Section 4.4.3 also demonstrates the practical inference efficiency.

# 3.4 Discussion

As the aforementioned analysis, compared with the Ghost-Net [17] and SineFM [18], the proposed GroupNL-based CNN design method has two highlights:

(i) More diverse feature maps. Given the seed filters and the number of output channels of the layer, by grouping the seed filters, more different hyperparameter settings of dataagnostic NLFs can be used to generate diverse features.

(ii) More efficient training. GroupNL generates the number of feature maps without extra computational nodes like cheap Conv and BN, effectively reducing the number of gradients exchanging in the multi-GPUs training.

# 4.1.1 Datasets

We evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed method using five widely used datasets. CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-1K are standard datasets. CIFAR-10-C, Icons-50, and ImageNet-C are used as corrupted datasets.

CIFAR-10 [39] is a multi-class natural object dataset for image classification. It consists of 50,000 training images and 10,000 test images in 10 categories, each with a resolution of 32×32 pixels.

CIFAR-10-C [40] is a test dataset after using synthetic common perturbations and noise corruptions on the CIFAR-10 test set. It consists of 10,000 test images of 19 types of damage in 4 categories. The resolution of each image is 32× 32 pixels.

Icons-50 [40] consists of 10,000 images in 50 categories collected from different companies. We set the resolution of all images to 32 × 32 pixels. For training, the data of one company is retained as test data, and the data of the rest of the companies is used as training data.

Please refer to [12] for more explanation of the CIFAR-10-C and Icons-50 datasets.

ImageNet-1K [41] is a large-scale classification dataset to verify computer vision performance. ImageNet-1K is a subset of the ILSVRC ImageNet, which contains 1.28 billion training images and 50K validation images from 1K different classes. The resolution of each image is 224×224 pixels.

ImageNet-C [40] is a large-scale dataset designed to evaluate the robustness of CNNs against common corruptions. The ImageNet-C includes 19 types of corruptions at five severity levels (as shown in Figure 4) in 5 categories: Noise, Blur, Weather, Digital, and Extra, simulating real-world challenges, $\mathrm { e . g . }$ , environmental conditions, based on ImageNet-1K validation data. The 19 types include: gaussian noise, shot noise, impulse noise, defocus blur, glass blur, motion blur, zoom blur, snow, frost, fog, brightness, contrast, elastic transformation, pixelate, JPEG compression, speckle noise, gaussian blur, spatter, and saturate. The resolution of the corrupted image is 224×224.

# 4.4.1 Selection of Nonlinear Functions

Choosing appropriate nonlinear transformations and selecting appropriate hyperparameters for the NLF is highly related to the performance of the GroupNL model.

Nonlinear Functions: It is worth noting that vanilla convolution operations utilize nonlinear functions for activation, which motivates us to consider the power of nonlinear transformation in feature map generation. Theoretically, all nonlinear functions can be used as NLFs in GroupNL to generate other feature maps based on the seed feature maps.

Referring to the aforementioned research about nonlinear transformations [12], [18], we select four different optimal nonlinear functions verified in these works, including the Sinusoidal function, Monomial function, Gaussian, and Laplace. We offer a conjecture: due to the periodicity of the Sinusoidal function and its different frequencies and phase hyperparameters, it can generate a richer variety of “similar but phase-different patterns” compared to other functions, which meets the requirement of GroupNL to generate similar but diverse feature maps. The reasons why other functions are worse may be: Monomial only performs power transformations and does not generate new spatial patterns, resulting in insufficient diversity; Gaussian function has too strong locality, with values only near the function center, and other values are suppressed to 0, which cannot guarantee the similarity of the generated images; Laplace function is similar to Gaussian.

# 5 CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK

This paper presented a low-resource and robust CNN design method, GroupNL. In GroupNL, seed feature maps are grouped and nonlinear transformation functions configured with different hyperparameters and lightweight tensor manipulation operations are used to generate diverse feature maps on demand to reduce the number of parameters and FLOPs. Experimental results show that GroupNL can effectively improve the robustness of CNNs while acceleration the training and inference. We will analyze the selection of NLFs from a theoretical perspective in our future work. Specifically, we plan to analyze the selection of NLFs from a theoretical perspective based function approximation theory, and through more fine-grained experimental analysis, such as analyzing the distance of the feature maps generated by each NLF.
